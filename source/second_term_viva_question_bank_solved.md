# Second Term Viva Question Bank — Solved

## 1. Environment and Health

### Environment

#### Q.1

**Question:**
Define environment. Classify Environment with examples.

**Answer:**
**Definition:** Environment is "all that which is external to the individual human host" – i.e., the sum total of all external forces, influences and conditions (living and non-living) that affect the life, growth, health and survival of man. It includes everything that is not part of the person's genetic make-up.

**Classification:**
1. **Internal environment** – the milieu intérieur: every individual's own tissues, organs, organ systems and their functions (e.g., blood pressure, blood glucose, hormones, immune status).
2. **External environment** – everything outside the human body:
   - **a. Physical environment:** air, water, soil, housing, climate, geography, heat, light, noise, radiation, waste (e.g., arsenic in tube-well water, air pollution, flooding).
   - **b. Biological environment:** living agents – viruses, bacteria, fungi, parasites, insects (vectors), rodents, animals, plants (e.g., Aedes mosquito, Vibrio cholerae, zoonotic reservoirs).
   - **c. Psychosocial environment:** cultural values, customs, habits, beliefs, morals, religion, education, lifestyle, community life, family structure, income, politics, urbanisation, stress (e.g., early marriage, tobacco/jarda use, food taboos in pregnancy).

### Water

#### Q.2

**Question:**
Mention the daily requirement of Water for an adult. Mention the uses of Water.

**Answer:**
**Daily requirement:** An adult needs about **2–3 litres/day as drinking water** (survival need). The total **domestic/community requirement is about 150–200 litres per capita per day (lpcd)** for an urban population (drinking, cooking, bathing, washing, flushing, public use); a minimum of about 40 lpcd is regarded as the bare minimum in rural settings. Bangladesh planning norms are lower than the urban figure in rural areas (DPHE targets, e.g., one hand tube-well per ~50 people in rural areas).

**Uses of water:**
1. **Domestic:** drinking, cooking, bathing, washing clothes/utensils, flushing latrines, house cleaning.
2. **Public:** street washing, fire-fighting, public fountains, parks and gardens, public latrines, hospitals/schools.
3. **Industrial:** cooling, processing, boilers, textile/garment/leather and food industries (very important in Bangladesh – RMG sector).
4. **Agricultural:** irrigation of crops (Boro rice), livestock, fisheries.
5. **Recreational:** swimming pools, boating, aquatic sports.
6. Also: power generation (hydroelectric – Kaptai), transport (inland waterways).

#### Q.3

**Question:**
State the sources of water. Mention the criteria of safe and wholesome water.

**Answer:**
**Sources of water:**
1. **Rain water** – purest natural source but collects impurities from air/roof; scanty/irregular; rain-water harvesting is used in coastal/arsenic-affected areas of Bangladesh.
2. **Surface water** – rivers/streams, tanks/ponds/lakes, impounded reservoirs, sea; always liable to pollution; must be treated (sedimentation, filtration, chlorination).
3. **Ground water** – shallow wells, deep wells (tube-wells), springs/artesian wells; clear and usually free from pathogens but may contain chemicals (arsenic, iron, salinity, fluoride, manganese). *Bangladesh:* >95% of the population uses tube-well water (mostly shallow hand tube-wells); arsenic contamination is the major problem; Dhaka WASA uses ground water + surface water (Saidabad, Padma-Jashaldia treatment plants).

**Criteria of safe and wholesome water:**
1. Free from pathogenic agents.
2. Free from harmful chemical substances.
3. Pleasant to the taste, i.e., free from colour and odour.
4. Usable for domestic purposes.

*(Bangladesh drinking-water standard – ECR/BDS: E. coli 0/100 ml; arsenic 0.05 mg/L national standard vs. WHO guideline 0.01 mg/L.)*

#### Q.4

**Question:**
Mention the criteria of safe and wholesome water.

**Answer:**
Safe and wholesome water must be:
1. **Free from pathogenic agents** (bacteria, viruses, protozoa, helminth ova – coliform and E. coli count nil in 100 ml).
2. **Free from harmful chemical substances** (arsenic, lead, fluoride, nitrates, pesticides within permissible limits).
3. **Pleasant to drink – free from colour, turbidity and odour** (aesthetically acceptable).
4. **Usable for domestic purposes** (i.e., suitable for drinking, cooking, bathing, washing).

Safe water = water that is free of harmful agents; wholesome water = safe water that is also palatable and fit for all domestic uses.

#### Q.5

**Question:**
Define water pollution and contamination. State the sources of water pollution. State the indicators of water pollution.

**Answer:**
**Water pollution:** the presence in water of foreign substances (organic, inorganic, biological or radiological) in quantities that degrade its quality and make it unfit/harmful for use.
**Contamination:** the presence in water of infectious agents, toxic chemicals or other harmful matter (e.g., faecal organisms, arsenic) that makes it unsafe for health — usually a specific hazardous agent; pollution is the wider degradation of quality.

**Sources of water pollution:**
1. **Sewage** (domestic/municipal) – human excreta, sullage.
2. **Industrial wastes** – chemicals, heavy metals (tanneries of Hazaribagh/Savar, textile dyeing, pharmaceuticals).
3. **Agricultural pollutants** – fertilisers, pesticides, animal wastes.
4. **Physical pollutants** – heat (thermal pollution from power plants), radioactive wastes.
5. **Oil pollution** – ships, ports (river and Bay of Bengal), accidents.
6. **Others** – solid-waste leachate, mining/ship-breaking, ballast water.

**Indicators of water pollution:**
- **Physical:** temperature, colour, odour, taste, turbidity.
- **Chemical:** chlorides, nitrogen compounds (free ammonia, albuminoid ammonia, nitrites, nitrates), dissolved oxygen (DO), biochemical oxygen demand (BOD), chemical oxygen demand (COD/permanganate value), pH, hardness.
- **Bacteriological:** coliform organisms (esp. *E. coli*), faecal streptococci, *Cl. perfringens*.
- **Biological:** presence of algae, plankton, protozoa, worms.

#### Q.6

**Question:**
E.coli is chosen as indicator of bacterial faecal pollution of water – Why?

**Answer:**
*E. coli* is the most important indicator of recent faecal pollution because:
1. It is a **normal inhabitant of the intestine of man and warm-blooded animals**, present in very large numbers (about 10⁸–10⁹ per gram of faeces) – so it is certain to be present whenever faecal pollution occurs.
2. It is **found almost exclusively in faeces** (not in unpolluted environments); presence = faecal contamination, and likely presence of enteric pathogens.
3. It is **easy to isolate and identify** by simple, cheap, quick methods (multiple tube/MPN, membrane filtration, presumptive coliform test).
4. It **survives in water slightly longer than most enteric pathogens**, but not for very long and does **not multiply** appreciably in water – hence it indicates *recent* pollution; its absence indicates pathogens are also absent.
5. It is **harmless in the quantity tested**, and survives similar to/longer than Salmonella/Shigella, being killed by the same chlorination, so its absence after treatment indicates adequate disinfection.

Standard: *E. coli* must be **0 per 100 ml** of drinking water. *Bangladesh:* national surveys (MICS 2019) show a high proportion of tube-well/household water samples contaminated with *E. coli*.

#### Q.7

**Question:**
Define Chlorination. What is Break Point Chlorination and Super Chlorination?

**Answer:**
**Chlorination:** addition of chlorine (as chlorine gas, bleaching powder/chlorinated lime, chlorine tablets, sodium hypochlorite) to water to **destroy pathogenic organisms** (bacteria, viruses, cysts) – the most widely used method of disinfection. Steps: (i) clarify water, (ii) determine **chlorine demand** (amount of chlorine consumed by organic matter/ammonia/microbes), (iii) add dose so that **free residual chlorine of 0.5 mg/L after contact time of 1 hour** is present, (iv) test with the orthotolidine (OT) or OTA test; dose determined by Horrock's apparatus (bleaching powder).

**Break-point chlorination:** As chlorine is added, first it is consumed by reducing agents (chlorine demand), then chloramines form and residual rises, then chloramines are destroyed (residual *falls*) and finally **free chlorine appears and residual rises again in direct proportion to the dose**. The point on the graph at which the residual starts to rise again, i.e., the **point at which the chlorine demand of the water is fully met and free residual chlorine begins to appear**, is the **break point**. Chlorination beyond the break point ensures complete disinfection and free residual chlorine.

**Super-chlorination:** Addition of **a heavy dose of chlorine** (much more than needed to meet the demand, giving a high free chlorine residual) to water, followed by **contact for at least 1 hour (or as specified) and then removal of excess chlorine (de-chlorination)** with sodium thiosulphate/sulphur dioxide/activated carbon/aeration before supply. Used for **emergencies – epidemics (cholera, hepatitis), floods, camps, during mass gatherings, grossly polluted water**, and for rapid disinfection of new wells/mains.

#### Q.8

**Question:**
Name some Water-borne Diseases.

**Answer:**
Water-borne diseases are caused when contaminated water is ingested:
- **Viral:** Hepatitis A, Hepatitis E, poliomyelitis, viral gastroenteritis (rotavirus, norovirus).
- **Bacterial:** Typhoid and paratyphoid fever, cholera, bacillary dysentery (shigellosis), *E. coli* diarrhoeas, leptospirosis.
- **Protozoal:** Amoebiasis, giardiasis.
- **Helminthic:** Ascariasis, hydatid disease.
- (Chemical: arsenicosis, fluorosis, methaemoglobinaemia – "water-borne" by chemical contamination.)

*Bangladesh:* diarrhoeal diseases, typhoid, hepatitis A/E and cholera are the major water-borne diseases; arsenicosis is the major chemical water hazard.

### Air and Ventilation

#### Q.10

**Question:**
Define Air Pollution. Mention the Air Pollutants. State the sources of Indoor Air Pollution.

**Answer:**
**Air pollution:** The presence in the outdoor atmosphere of one or more contaminants (dust, fumes, gas, mist, odour, smoke, vapour) in such quantities, with such characteristics and of such duration, as to be **injurious to human, plant or animal life or property, or which unreasonably interfere with the comfortable enjoyment of life and property**.

**Air pollutants:**
- **Gaseous:** carbon monoxide (CO), carbon dioxide (CO₂), sulphur dioxide (SO₂), oxides of nitrogen (NOx), ozone, hydrocarbons, hydrogen sulphide, ammonia, chlorofluorocarbons.
- **Particulate:** dust, soot, smoke, fumes, mists; **PM₁₀ and PM₂.₅**, fly ash, lead and other heavy metals, asbestos.
- **Biological/other:** pollen, spores, bacteria; radioactive substances.
*(Primary pollutants are emitted directly; secondary pollutants e.g., ozone, smog form in the atmosphere.)*

**Sources of indoor air pollution:**
1. **Combustion of biomass fuels** (wood, cow-dung, crop residues) and coal/kerosene for cooking and heating in poorly ventilated kitchens.
2. **Tobacco smoking** (passive/second-hand smoke).
3. **Building and household materials** – asbestos, formaldehyde (furniture, plywood), radon, paints, solvents.
4. **Insecticide/mosquito coils, aerosol sprays, cleaning agents, cosmetics.**
5. **Poor ventilation, overcrowding, dampness** – dust mites, moulds, bacteria, viruses.
6. Pets, cooking fumes, incense.

*Bangladesh:* Dhaka frequently ranks among the most polluted cities (PM₂.₅ far above WHO guideline) from brick kilns, vehicles, construction dust and industry. Indoor biomass smoke affects most rural households. Legal framework: Environment Conservation Act 1995 and Environment Conservation Rules (air quality standards); DoE under the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change.

#### Q.11

**Question:**
State the effects of air pollution. How will you prevent and control Air pollution?

**Answer:**
**Effects of air pollution:**
1. **Health effects:** *acute* – eye/throat irritation, cough, asthma attacks, pneumonia, acute bronchitis, increased mortality during smog episodes (London 1952, Bhopal 1984); *chronic* – chronic bronchitis, emphysema/COPD, lung cancer, cardiovascular disease, reduced lung function in children, adverse pregnancy outcomes, CO poisoning.
2. **Effects on vegetation/crops** and animals (acid rain, fluorides).
3. **Effects on materials/property** – corrosion, soiling, damage to monuments.
4. **Effects on climate/global environment** – global warming (greenhouse effect), ozone-layer depletion, acid rain, reduced visibility/sunlight.
5. **Economic effects** – cost of illness, lost workdays.

**Prevention and control:**
1. **Containment** – enclose the source of pollution: closed systems, filters, electrostatic precipitators, scrubbers, cyclones; use of cleaner production in industries.
2. **Replacement** – substitute polluting processes/fuels with non-polluting ones: smokeless fuel, electricity/LPG/biogas, improved cook-stoves, CNG vehicles, cleaner technology.
3. **Dilution** – tall chimneys, locating industries away from residential areas so pollutants are dispersed (provides only temporary relief).
4. **Legislation** – clean-air laws, emission standards, vehicle fitness testing, environmental clearance, zoning; Bangladesh: Environment Conservation Act 1995 and Rules, brick-kiln regulations (shift to zig-zag/HHK kilns).
5. **International action** – WHO air quality guidelines, Montreal Protocol, Paris Agreement.
Also: **health education**, urban planning/green belts, traffic management, monitoring (Continuous Air Monitoring Stations by DoE).

#### Q.12

**Question:**
Name the greenhouse gases. Mention the sources of greenhouse gases. Mention the consequences of the greenhouse effect.

**Answer:**
**Greenhouse gases:** carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane (CH₄), nitrous oxide (N₂O), chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) (also tropospheric ozone and water vapour; HFCs/PFCs/SF₆ in the Kyoto list).

**Sources:**
- **CO₂:** burning of fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas), power plants, vehicles, deforestation, cement industry.
- **Methane:** rice paddies, cattle/ruminants, landfills and waste decomposition, coal mining, natural gas leaks, wetlands.
- **Nitrous oxide:** nitrogen fertilisers, biomass burning, industrial processes, sewage.
- **CFCs:** refrigerators/air conditioners, aerosol sprays, foam plastics, solvents.

**Consequences of the greenhouse effect (global warming):**
1. Rise in global temperature, melting of glaciers/ice caps and **sea-level rise** (flooding of coastal areas, salinity intrusion).
2. **Climate change** – erratic rainfall, floods, droughts, cyclones, heat waves.
3. **Health effects** – heat stroke, cardiovascular/respiratory diseases, spread of vector-borne diseases (malaria, dengue), water-borne diseases, malnutrition/food insecurity, migration, mental stress.
4. Loss of agricultural productivity, biodiversity loss, economic losses.
*Bangladesh* is among the most climate-vulnerable countries (coastal salinity with hypertension in pregnancy, cyclones Sidr/Aila/Amphan, riverine flooding, climate migrants; National Adaptation Plan/Delta Plan 2100).

### Disposal of Solid Waste

#### Q.14

**Question:**
Define Sewage, Sullage and Garbage.

**Answer:**
- **Sewage:** the waste water from a community containing **human excreta (faeces and urine)** together with domestic/sullage water and industrial effluents and sometimes storm water; carried in sewers (≈99.9% water, 0.1% solids).
- **Sullage:** waste water from **kitchens, bathrooms, washing and laundry – it does not contain human excreta** (also called grey water); less polluting than sewage but still a nuisance/breeding place for mosquitoes.
- **Garbage:** the **putrescible (decomposable) solid waste** from preparation, cooking and serving of food – kitchen waste, vegetable/fruit peels, left-over food, market waste; it decays quickly, producing odour and attracting flies, rats and stray animals.

---

#### Q.15

**Question:**
Define Solid Waste. State the Health hazards of Solid Waste. State the sources of Refuse, Garbage and Rubbish.

**Answer:**
**Solid waste:** the **non-liquid, non-gaseous, discarded materials** produced by human and animal activities (domestic, commercial, industrial, agricultural, institutional) that are useless, unwanted or hazardous and need disposal; "refuse" is the general term covering all of garbage, rubbish, ashes, street sweepings, dead animals, industrial and construction waste.

**Health hazards:**
1. **Breeding of flies, mosquitoes, rodents and vermin** → diarrhoea, dysentery, typhoid, cholera, plague, leptospirosis, dengue.
2. **Pollution of water** (leachate contaminates surface/ground water), **soil and air** (burning, odour, methane).
3. **Direct hazards to workers/waste-pickers** – injuries (glass, needles), infections (tetanus, hepatitis B), respiratory and skin diseases.
4. **Nuisance/aesthetic problems**, blockage of drains → water-logging, urban flooding.
5. **Fire hazards and explosions** (methane); **toxic/hazardous wastes** (heavy metals, biomedical waste) → poisoning, cancers.

**Sources:**
- **Refuse (mixed solid waste):** households, streets (sweepings), markets, commercial establishments, institutions (hospitals, schools), industries, construction/demolition sites, agriculture.
- **Garbage:** kitchens, restaurants/hotels, canteens, food markets, vegetable/fish markets.
- **Rubbish:** homes, offices, shops, factories, construction sites (paper, cardboard, plastic, glass, metal, rags, wood, ashes).

*Bangladesh:* Dhaka alone generates several thousand tons of waste a day, with a large part uncollected or dumped in open sites (Matuail, Amin Bazar); policy base – 3R (Reduce-Reuse-Recycle) Strategy and Solid Waste Management Rules; Medical Waste Management Rules 2008 for hospital waste.

#### Q.16

**Question:**
State the methods of Refuse Disposal. What is Controlled Tipping?

**Answer:**
**Methods of refuse disposal:**
1. **Dumping** – refuse dumped in low-lying areas/open land (crude; creates nuisance, fly/rat breeding).
2. **Controlled tipping (sanitary landfill)**
3. **Incineration** – burning at high temperature in incinerators (suitable for hospital waste).
4. **Composting** – aerobic/anaerobic decomposition to manure (Bangalore method, Indore method, mechanical composting).
5. **Manure pits** – pits for rural households to convert garbage/animal dung to manure.
6. **Burial** – refuse buried in trenches (for small communities/camps).

**Controlled tipping (sanitary landfill):** the **best method of refuse disposal** for towns/cities when land is available. Refuse is dumped into a prepared **trench or pit/low-lying area, compacted in layers about 1.5 m thick, and covered each day with a layer of earth (about 20–30 cm)**, with a final earth cover when full. The earth cover prevents flies, rodents, odour and fire; decomposition (aerobic then anaerobic, over ~4–6 months) converts it to harmless humus; leachate and gas (methane) are controlled/collected. Methods: **trench method, ramp method, area method**. Disadvantages: needs adequate land, leachate may pollute ground water if site not chosen properly.

### Excreta Disposal

#### Q.17

**Question:**
State the methods of Excreta Disposal. State the criteria of Sanitary Latrine. Define Sanitation Barrier.

**Answer:**
**Methods of excreta disposal:**
- **A. Unsewered areas**
  - *Service type* (conservancy) – bucket latrine (insanitary; obsolete).
  - *Non-service (sanitary) type:* 1. Dry pit/trench latrine, 2. **Bore-hole latrine**, 3. **Water-seal (pour-flush) latrine** (PRAI/RCA/ sanitary slab with water seal), 4. **Septic tank**, 5. **Aqua-privy**, 6. **Cesspool**; communal/public latrines.
- **B. Sewered areas:** water-carriage system – flush latrines connected to **sewerage system and sewage treatment** (primary, secondary, tertiary treatment; oxidation ponds).

**Criteria of a sanitary latrine:**
1. The surface soil is **not contaminated**.
2. **No contamination of ground water.**
3. **No contamination of surface water.**
4. Excreta are **not accessible to flies, rodents, animals** and insects.
5. **No handling of fresh excreta** is necessary.
6. **No odours or unsightly conditions.**
7. Design is **simple and inexpensive** in construction and operation.

**Sanitation barrier:** a **barrier that is interposed between human excreta (the source of infection) and the host**, preventing the transmission of faecal-borne diseases by breaking the faeco-oral chain. The **primary barrier** is hygienic isolation/disposal of faeces (sanitary latrine); **secondary barriers** (protection of water supply, food hygiene, personal/hand hygiene, fly control) block spread to the next host – "F-diagram": faeces, fingers, flies, fluids, fields, food.

*Bangladesh:* open defecation has fallen to below 1%; most households use improved/basic sanitation, but safely-managed sanitation (faecal sludge management) is a key gap. Programmes: community-led total sanitation (CLTS), National Sanitation Strategy, DPHE and LGD (Local Government Division) as lead agencies.

## 2. Public Health Administration and Management

#### Q.18

**Question:**
Mention the Levels of Health Care.

**Answer:**
There are **three levels of health care**:
1. **Primary health care (first level)** – first contact of the individual/community with the health system; essential, community-based, affordable care by health workers/doctors at the periphery (sub-centre, health centre).
2. **Secondary health care (second level)** – more complex care for patients referred from primary level; provided by district/sub-district hospitals and community hospitals with basic specialist services (medicine, surgery, obstetrics, paediatrics) and diagnostics.
3. **Tertiary health care (third level)** – highly specialised care for referred cases by regional/central institutions: medical college hospitals, specialised and post-graduate institutes, super-specialty centres.

*Bangladesh (how the levels map):* **Primary** – Community Clinics (1 per ~6,000 population, run by Community Health Care Providers), Union Health & Family Welfare Centres, Union Sub-Centres; **Secondary** – Upazila Health Complexes (UHC, 31–50 beds), District/General Hospitals, Maternal and Child Welfare Centres; **Tertiary** – medical college hospitals, specialised institutes/hospitals (BSMMU, NICVD, NITOR, NICRH, NIKDU etc.). Administrative structure: Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (Health Services Division; Medical Education and Family Welfare Division) – DGHS and DGFP at directorate level; divisional, district, upazila and union levels below.

---

#### Q.19

**Question:**
…the Elements of Primary Health Care. Mention the …

**Answer:**
**Elements of Primary Health Care** (Alma-Ata Declaration 1978 – eight essential elements):
1. **Education** concerning prevailing health problems and the methods of preventing and controlling them.
2. **Promotion of food supply and proper nutrition.**
3. **Adequate supply of safe water and basic sanitation.**
4. **Maternal and child health care, including family planning.**
5. **Immunisation** against the major infectious diseases.
6. **Prevention and control of locally endemic diseases.**
7. **Appropriate treatment of common diseases and injuries.**
8. **Provision of essential drugs.**

*Bangladesh:* PHC is delivered through the Essential Services Package (ESP) at community clinics, union health centres and UHCs; Bangladesh has also adopted PHC-based Universal Health Coverage (UHC) as the goal in its Health, Population and Nutrition Sector Programme (HPNSP).

---

#### Q.20

**Question:**
…principles of primary health care. Mention the activities of …

**Answer:**
**Principles of Primary Health Care:**
1. **Equitable distribution** – health services should be shared equally by all people irrespective of caste, creed, colour, rich or poor, urban or rural ("health for all").
2. **Community participation** – individuals and families assume responsibility for their own health and welfare; involvement in planning, organisation, operation and control.
3. **Intersectoral coordination** – PHC involves other sectors (agriculture, education, housing, communication, rural development, etc.) besides health.
4. **Appropriate technology** – technology that is scientifically sound, adaptable to local needs, acceptable to the community and affordable (maintainable, locally manufactured when possible).

*Bangladesh:* these principles are operationalised through community clinics with Community Support Groups (CSGs) and Community Groups (CGs) – community participation – and through coordination with LGD/Union Parishad, education, and agriculture sectors.

#### Q.21

**Question:**
Explain Multisectoral approach in relation to Primary Health Care (PHC).

**Answer:**
**Concept:** Health is influenced by many factors beyond the health sector – food, water, education, housing, income, environment – so improvement of health demands **coordinated action by all related sectors**, not by the health sector alone. This is the PHC principle of **intersectoral (multisectoral) coordination**.

**Sectors involved and their roles:**
- **Agriculture / Food and Animal husbandry:** food production, food security, nutrition (fisheries, poultry, vegetable gardening), control of zoonoses.
- **Education:** school health, health education, literacy (esp. female education), nutrition education, school feeding.
- **Public works/Water supply and sanitation:** safe water, latrines, drainage, waste disposal.
- **Housing, Communication/Transport, Industry:** housing standards, roads for referral/emergency transport, occupational health.
- **Rural development / Local government:** community development, sanitation, health volunteers.
- **Social welfare / Women's affairs:** women's empowerment, nutrition and child care, welfare services.
- **Mass media / Information:** health communication.

**Requirement for success:** a coordinating mechanism (committees at upazila/district levels), shared goals, sharing of resources, joint planning and monitoring, and involvement of the community and NGOs.

**Examples (Bangladesh):** DPHE (water, sanitation) and LGD; Ministry of Primary and Mass Education/Ministry of Education (school health, school feeding); Ministry of Agriculture/Fisheries & Livestock (nutrition-sensitive agriculture, food safety, One Health for zoonoses/rabies/avian influenza); Ministry of Women & Children Affairs; Ministry of Environment (climate/air); Union Parishad–Upazila coordination committees; NGOs (BRAC, icddr,b, Grameen) – the **Multisectoral Action Plan for NCD** and National Nutrition Services also follow this approach.

### Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs)

#### Q.22

**Question:**
Define Non-communicable disease. Mention some important differences between communicable and non-communicable diseases.

**Answer:**
**Non-communicable disease (NCD):** a disease or condition that is **not caused by an infectious agent and is not transmitted from person to person**; typically of **long duration, slow progression, and multifactorial causation** (lifestyle, genetic, environmental and behavioural factors). Major NCDs: cardiovascular diseases, cancers, chronic respiratory diseases, diabetes (+ mental health, injuries, etc.).

**Differences:**

| Feature | Communicable diseases | Non-communicable diseases |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Specific living agent (bacteria, virus, parasite) – usually a single agent | Multifactorial – many risk factors (lifestyle, genetics, environment) |
| Transmission | Transmitted from person to person/vector/vehicle | Not transmitted |
| Onset | Usually acute/sudden; short incubation period | Insidious, long latent period |
| Course | Short duration; self-limiting or curable | Chronic, prolonged, often irreversible; need long-term care |
| Epidemiology | Epidemics/outbreaks, endemicity; agent identifiable | No epidemics (but "epidemic" of rising burden); risk-factor based |
| Immunity | Acquired immunity common | Generally no immunity |
| Prevention | Vaccination, sanitation, vector control, isolation | Lifestyle modification, early screening, health promotion, risk-factor control |
| Diagnosis | Laboratory identification of agent | Clinical, biochemical, imaging; no single agent |
| Surveillance | Notifiable; contact tracing | Population surveys (STEPS), registries |

#### Q.23

**Question:**
Give examples of NCDs. Characteristics of NCDs. Define and Classify risk factors of disease.

**Answer:**
**Examples:** hypertension, ischaemic heart disease, stroke, diabetes mellitus, cancers, COPD, bronchial asthma, obesity, chronic kidney disease, rheumatic heart disease, mental illness, accidents/injuries, blindness, osteoporosis.

**Characteristics:**
1. **Multifactorial causation** – several interacting risk factors.
2. **Long latent period** and **insidious onset**; no definite onset.
3. **Prolonged/chronic course** with a gradual progression and often irreversible damage.
4. **Non-infectious – no specific agent; no immunity.**
5. **Poorly defined etiology, complicated by lifestyle/environment factors**; lack of specific treatment and need for long-term management; high cost to the individual/health system.
6. Common in adults and elderly but increasingly in the young; major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide and in Bangladesh (about two-thirds of deaths).

**Risk factor:** an **attribute or exposure that is associated with an increased probability of a specified outcome (disease)**; it is not necessarily a causal factor (a marker).

**Classification of risk factors:**
1. **Non-modifiable (fixed):** age, sex, genetic/family history, race/ethnicity.
2. **Modifiable (behavioural):** tobacco use, harmful use of alcohol, unhealthy diet (high salt, fat, low fruit/vegetable), physical inactivity.
3. **Intermediate/metabolic (physiological) risk factors:** raised blood pressure, raised blood glucose, raised lipids, overweight/obesity.
4. **Environmental/social determinants:** air pollution, occupation, poverty, urbanisation, education, stress.
*(Other classification: personal/ environmental; primary/ secondary; direct/indirect.)*

*Bangladesh:* NCDs cause about two-thirds of deaths; key programmes – "National Strategic Plan for Surveillance and Prevention and Control of NCDs", **Multisectoral Action Plan for NCDs**, NCD Corners at upazila health complexes, WHO PEN package, STEPS survey; tobacco use in adults is about 35% (GATS 2017).

### Communicable Diseases (CDs)

#### Q.26

**Question:**
Define Zoonosis with examples. What is Epizootic and Enzootic?

**Answer:**
**Zoonosis:** an infection or infectious disease **transmissible under natural conditions from vertebrate animals to man**.

**Classification and examples:**
1. **Direct zoonoses** (by contact, bite, droplets, ingestion – one vertebrate host): rabies, brucellosis, anthrax, bovine TB, leptospirosis, Q fever, **Nipah**/avian influenza.
2. **Cyclo-zoonoses** (need more than one vertebrate host, no invertebrate): taeniasis (Taenia solium/saginata), hydatid disease, pentastomid infection.
3. **Meta-zoonoses** (need invertebrate and vertebrate hosts): plague, Japanese encephalitis, KFD, yellow fever, leishmaniasis (zoonotic forms).
4. **Sapro-zoonoses** (need a non-animal reservoir like soil/plants): histoplasmosis, cryptococcosis, larva migrans.

*Bangladesh:* rabies, anthrax, brucellosis, leptospirosis, Nipah virus (date-palm sap, Rajbari/Faridpur belt), avian influenza (H5N1/H9N2), JE – addressed with the **One Health** approach (IEDCR, DLS, DGHS).

**Epizootic:** an **outbreak/epidemic of disease in an animal population** (excess occurrence above the normal – animal equivalent of epidemic), e.g., anthrax or avian influenza outbreak in poultry/cattle.
**Enzootic:** a disease **constantly present in an animal population within a given area** (animal equivalent of endemic), e.g., brucellosis in cattle herds, rabies in dogs.

#### Q.28 (CASE)

**Question:**
An old person with history of road-traffic accident has come to you with Lock Jaw and Convulsion. What is your diagnosis? What preventive measures would have been taken to prevent this disease?

**Answer:**
**Diagnosis: Tetanus** (trismus/lock-jaw with muscle spasms/convulsions following a contaminated wound – RTA). Caused by exotoxin (tetanospasmin) of *Clostridium tetani*; incubation 3–21 days; features: trismus, risus sardonicus, opisthotonus, spasms triggered by stimuli, clear sensorium.

**Preventive measures that should have been taken:**
1. **Active immunisation:** complete primary series with DPT/pentavalent in infancy and **Td/TT boosters** (Bangladesh EPI: Td in school children and in pregnancy; adults who were never immunised need 2 doses 4 weeks apart + booster at 6–12 months). Old persons are often not immunised.
2. **Proper wound care:** immediate cleaning, removal of foreign bodies/dead tissue (surgical toilet/debridement), no suturing of contaminated wounds, hydrogen-peroxide/antiseptic wash.
3. **Passive immunisation at the time of injury:** **human tetanus immunoglobulin (TIG) 250 IU IM** for tetanus-prone wounds in non-immunised/unknown status persons – given at a *different site* from toxoid.
4. **Tetanus toxoid booster:** immunised person with a dirty wound and last dose >5 years ago (clean wound >10 years) → one booster dose; not immunised → full course with TIG.
5. **Antibiotics** (penicillin/metronidazole) as prophylaxis in severe wounds.
6. **Health education** and road-safety measures.

**Management (brief):** hospital admission (ICU), quiet dark room, **TIG 3,000–6,000 IU** IM, metronidazole, diazepam/muscle relaxants, airway care, wound toilet, nutrition, active immunisation after recovery.
*Bangladesh:* achieved **maternal and neonatal tetanus elimination in 2008**; adult/injury tetanus still occurs.

#### Q.29 (CASE)

**Question:**
A patient has come to you with complaints of Low Grade Fever, Cough for more than 4 weeks and Haemoptysis. What is your Probable Diagnosis? Name the investigations will you suggest for confirmation of the diagnosis.

**Answer:**
**Probable diagnosis: Pulmonary tuberculosis** (chronic cough >2 weeks, evening rise of low-grade fever, haemoptysis, weight loss/night sweats).

**Investigations:**
1. **Sputum examination** – smear microscopy for AFB (Ziehl–Neelsen or fluorescence), two (spot + early morning) samples.
2. **Xpert MTB/RIF (GeneXpert)** – rapid molecular test detecting *M. tuberculosis* and rifampicin resistance (used as the primary diagnostic test in the Bangladesh NTP along with microscopy).
3. **Sputum culture** (Löwenstein–Jensen/MGIT liquid) with drug-sensitivity testing (DST); line-probe assay for MDR-TB.
4. **Chest X-ray** (PA view) – upper lobe infiltrates, cavitation, fibrosis; digital X-ray with CAD software used for screening.
5. **Supportive:** ESR (raised), CBC (lymphocytosis), Mantoux test (tuberculin; positive ≥10 mm; limited value in endemic areas), HIV test, blood sugar (diabetes–TB), liver function before treatment.
6. CT chest, bronchoscopy/BAL for difficult cases.

*Bangladesh:* one of the 30 high-burden TB countries with an incidence of roughly 200+ per 100,000; National TB Control Programme (NTP, DGHS) provides free diagnosis and treatment.

#### Q.30

**Question:**
DOTS strategy is adopted in our country to control TB – why?

**Answer:**
DOTS (Directly Observed Treatment, Short-course) was adopted because:
1. **Tuberculosis is a major public health problem** in Bangladesh (high-burden country) – a large reservoir of infection (sputum-positive patients) sustains transmission.
2. **Ensures completion of treatment** – a health worker/volunteer watches the patient swallow every dose, preventing default/irregular treatment.
3. **High cure rates** (>90% treatment success in Bangladesh) – reducing the pool of infectious cases and thereby **interrupting transmission**.
4. **Prevents drug resistance (MDR-TB)** – irregular treatment with single drugs breeds resistant bacilli.
5. **Short-course regimen (6–8 months) with fixed-dose combinations** – cheaper, better compliance, fewer relapses than long regimens.
6. **Systematic case-finding, standard regimen, uninterrupted drug supply and standard recording/reporting** → measurable outcomes, accountability.
7. **Cost-effective** – one of the most cost-effective health interventions.
8. Decentralised delivery – diagnosis and treatment at community clinics/UHCs/NGO sites with community involvement.
*(Bangladesh NTP adopted DOTS in the 1990s and expanded it nationwide; the WHO End TB Strategy now builds upon it.)*

#### Q.31

**Question:**
What is DOTS?

**Answer:**
**DOTS = Directly Observed Treatment, Short-course** – the WHO-recommended strategy for TB control in which a **trained observer (health worker/community volunteer/CHCP) directly watches the patient taking every dose of anti-TB drugs** until cure.

**Five components of DOTS:**
1. **Political and administrative commitment** (government commitment with funding).
2. **Case detection by quality-assured sputum-smear microscopy** among symptomatic (chest symptomatics) patients attending health services.
3. **Standardised short-course chemotherapy under direct observation** (6–8 months).
4. **Uninterrupted supply of quality-assured anti-TB drugs.**
5. **Standardised recording and reporting system** (monitoring treatment outcomes: cure, completed, failure, default, death, transfer-out).

**Treatment:** New patients – **2 months intensive phase (H, R, Z, E) followed by 4 months continuation phase (H, R)**, daily fixed-dose combinations (Bangladesh NTP, adjusted for weight band); DR-TB gets shorter oral regimens.

#### Q.32 (CASE)

**Question:**
A person has come to you with hypo-pigmented skin patches with loss of sensation. What is your diagnosis? Mention its types. Name the drugs for treatment.

**Answer:**
**Diagnosis: Leprosy** (*Mycobacterium leprae*) – hypopigmented/erythematous anaesthetic patches ± thickened peripheral nerves ± AFB on skin smear.

**Types:**
- **Ridley–Jopling (histological/immunological) classification:** Tuberculoid (TT), Borderline tuberculoid (BT), Mid-borderline (BB), Borderline lepromatous (BL), Lepromatous (LL); plus indeterminate leprosy (early).
- **WHO operational classification (for treatment):**
  - **Paucibacillary (PB):** 1–5 skin lesions, no bacilli on skin smear.
  - **Multibacillary (MB):** more than 5 lesions, or nerve involvement/positive smear.

**Drugs (multidrug therapy – MDT, WHO):**
- **MB:** rifampicin 600 mg once monthly (supervised) + clofazimine 300 mg monthly (supervised) and 50 mg daily + dapsone 100 mg daily – **12 months**.
- **PB:** rifampicin 600 mg monthly (supervised) + dapsone 100 mg daily – **6 months** (WHO 2018 additionally advises adding clofazimine for PB; follow current NLEP protocol).
- Reactions managed with prednisolone/thalidomide (where permitted)/clofazimine; prevention of disability/deformity and rehabilitation.

*Bangladesh:* leprosy reached the elimination target (<1 case per 10,000 population) at national level, but pockets remain (north-western districts); National Leprosy Elimination Programme (NLEP, DGHS) with support from Damien Foundation, German Leprosy & TB Relief Association, etc.

**Question:**
State the control measure of Covid-19.

**Answer:**
Control of COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2) –:
1. **Control of the source of infection:** early case detection (case definition), **testing (RT-PCR/antigen)**, **isolation** of cases, contact tracing and **quarantine** of contacts, treatment (oxygen, steroids, antivirals/monoclonal antibodies as indicated).

#### Q.34 (CASE)

**Question:**
A man has come to you with a history of recent dog bite. He has informed you that he was vaccinated previously with anti-rabies vaccines 6 months ago when he was bitten by another dog. What will be your advice for him regarding anti-rabies vaccine this time?

**Answer:**
**Principles:** he was fully vaccinated with a potent cell-culture vaccine previously, so he has immunological memory – he needs only **booster doses and no rabies immunoglobulin (RIG)**, *provided the earlier course was complete and with a modern cell-culture vaccine*.

**Advice:**
1. **Immediate wound care:** wash the wound thoroughly with soap and running water for **≥15 minutes**, apply antiseptic (povidone-iodine); avoid suturing/tight dressing; tetanus toxoid and antibiotics as needed.
2. **Classify the exposure** (Category I/II/III) and observe/assess the biting dog (10-day observation if domestic dog; stray/unknown dog – treat as suspect).
3. **Vaccine:** **2 booster doses on day 0 and day 3** (intramuscular, or equivalent intradermal schedule per national guidelines). **No RIG** is required because he has been previously fully immunised (within the booster window; if the earlier course was incomplete/with nerve-tissue vaccine, treat as non-immunised with full course + RIG for category III).
4. Counsel on completing the doses, reporting symptoms of rabies (hydrophobia, agitation), and about the future bite risk; pet/dog vaccination.

*Bangladesh:* post-exposure prophylaxis (cell-culture vaccine, intradermal regimen, and RIG for category III) is available free at designated centres (Dhaka Infectious Disease Hospital, district and upazila hospitals under the National Rabies Elimination Programme, aiming at rabies elimination by 2030 with mass dog vaccination).

#### Q.35

**Question:**
Kala-azar is endemic in Trishal area – explain?

**Answer:**
**Kala-azar (visceral leishmaniasis)** is caused by *Leishmania donovani*, transmitted by the female sand-fly **Phlebotomus argentipes**; man is the only reservoir (anthroponotic; PKDL cases are a reservoir). Bangladesh's highest burden occurs in **Mymensingh division (Trishal, Fulbaria, Bhaluka, Gaffargaon, Muktagacha etc.)**, contiguous to endemic Bihar (India).

**Why Trishal is endemic (host–agent–environment):**
1. **Environment:** **alluvial soil, high humidity, high ground-water level, dense vegetation, cattle sheds and mud-plastered houses with cracks/crevices** – ideal breeding/resting sites for sand-flies (organic matter, moist soil).
2. **Vector:** abundant *P. argentipes*, biting at night; fogs/rainy season (monsoon) increase density.
3. **Host factors:** poverty, **malnutrition/low immunity**, poor housing (mud walls, thatched roofs), sleeping on the floor, close proximity to cattle; poor awareness; delayed diagnosis.
4. **Reservoir:** untreated kala-azar and post-kala-azar dermal leishmaniasis (PKDL) cases maintain transmission.
5. Agricultural activities, rural settlement pattern; and cross-border movement from endemic areas of India.

**Control (Bangladesh Kala-azar Elimination Programme):** early diagnosis (**rK39 rapid test**), treatment (**single-dose liposomal amphotericin-B 10 mg/kg** as first line; miltefosine), vector control (indoor residual spray with deltamethrin/alpha-cypermethrin, insecticide-treated nets), PKDL case management, health education, active case search. *Bangladesh was **validated by WHO (October 2023)** as the first country to eliminate kala-azar as a public health problem (<1 case per 10,000 population at upazila level)*; surveillance must be sustained.

#### Q.36

**Question:**
State the principles of Prevention and Control of Malaria.

**Answer:**
**Principles:**
1. **Control/elimination of the reservoir (parasite) – case management:** early diagnosis (microscopy/**rapid diagnostic tests**) and **prompt, radical treatment** (artemisinin-based combination therapy – ACT; primaquine for *P. vivax*/gametocytes), surveillance and case investigation, treating all cases to eliminate gametocytes.
2. **Interruption of transmission – vector control:**
   - **Anti-adult measures:** indoor residual spraying (IRS), space spraying, insecticide-treated/long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs).
   - **Anti-larval measures:** source reduction (drainage, filling), larvicides, larvivorous fish (Gambusia), environmental management.
   - **Personal protection:** bed nets, repellents, protective clothing, screening of houses.
3. **Protection of the susceptible – chemoprophylaxis** (for pregnant women, travellers, non-immune persons) and (WHO-recommended RTS,S/R21 vaccines for children in endemic areas).
4. **Health education and community participation**, intersectoral action (forestry, agriculture, education, irrigation).
5. **Surveillance, monitoring and research** (resistance, vector bionomics) and capacity building.

*Bangladesh:* **National Malaria Elimination Programme (NMEP, DGHS)**; target: elimination by 2030; transmission concentrated in the **hill districts (Bandarban, Rangamati, Khagrachhari) and Cox's Bazar**, with a few north-eastern border areas; strategy – LLIN distribution, community case management via CHCPs/BRAC, RDT/ACT free of cost.

**Question:**
Malaria is an endemic disease in our country – Why?

**Answer:**
Malaria remains endemic in Bangladesh (especially the 13 districts bordering India and Myanmar, and the Chattogram Hill Tracts) because of:

7. Gaps in surveillance and vector control; climate change.

#### Q.40 (CASE)

**Question:**
A patient with diarrhoea has come to you. On examination the eyes were very sunken, unable to drink and the skin pinch goes back very slowly. Comment about Dehydration Status. How will you manage the case?

**Answer:**
**Dehydration status: SEVERE dehydration** (WHO/IMCI: two or more of – lethargic/unconscious, sunken eyes, unable to drink/drinking poorly, skin pinch goes back *very slowly* (>2 seconds)). This is **Plan C**.

**Management (WHO Plan C):**
1. **Start intravenous fluid immediately – Ringer's lactate (or normal saline)**, **100 ml/kg total**:
   - Infants (<12 months): **30 ml/kg in the first 1 hour**, then **70 ml/kg over the next 5 hours**.
   - Children/adults (≥12 months): **30 ml/kg in the first 30 minutes**, then **70 ml/kg in the next 2½ hours**.
2. If the patient can drink – **give ORS (5 ml/kg/hour) by mouth/NG tube simultaneously**.
3. **Reassess every 15–30 minutes**; if hydration not improving, give IV more rapidly. After full IV, **reassess**: then choose Plan A, B or C.
4. **Zinc supplementation** (10 mg/day <6 months; 20 mg/day ≥6 months for 10–14 days) in children.
5. **Antibiotics:** only for suspected cholera with severe dehydration (e.g., azithromycin/doxycycline/ciprofloxacin per age) and for dysentery (bloody stool) – otherwise none for acute watery diarrhoea.
6. **Continue feeding/breastfeeding**; avoid anti-motility agents; monitor urine output; treat complications (hypokalaemia, acidosis, hypoglycaemia).
7. **Refer/hospitalise** if IV cannot be given; keep for observation at least 6 hours; **educate** on safe water, hand-washing, food hygiene, ORS preparation, reporting of cases (outbreak control).
*Bangladesh:* icddr,b (Dhaka) pioneered ORS (Matlab 1968, Lancet "potentially the most important medical advance of the century") and the cholera cot; DGHS/ICDDR,B guidelines on "Dhaka solution" – treatment is **free** at government hospitals and icddr,b.

## 4. MCH-FP and Demography

### Maternal and Child Health (MCH)

#### Q.41

**Question:**
Mother and Child are considered as one unit – Explain.

**Answer:**
Mother and child form a **biological and social unit** because:
1. **Biological dependence:** the foetus grows within the mother; **maternal health, nutrition, infection, age and parity affect birth weight, prematurity, congenital anomalies and perinatal/neonatal mortality**; the neonate depends on breast milk for nutrition and immunity.
2. **Health of the child begins before conception** – the mother's pre-pregnancy and antenatal condition determine pregnancy outcome; the **child's health in turn affects maternal health** (frequent pregnancies, lactation, child illness stress).
3. **Maternal and child health problems are inter-related** – malnutrition, anaemia, low birth weight cycle, infections, birth spacing; maternal death increases child mortality (orphans).
4. **Social unit:** mother is the main caregiver – feeding, hygiene, immunisation, child development, psychosocial stimulation and decisions on health care depend upon her education and status.
5. **Vulnerable (special-risk) groups:** mothers and children together make up **≈ 60–70% of the population in developing countries** (children <15 about one-quarter in Bangladesh plus women of reproductive age) and account for most of the disease burden; they are the **most in need of preventive health care**.
6. **Service integration:** care is therefore delivered together – **antenatal, intranatal, postnatal, immunisation, family planning, nutrition (MCH-FP services)**; in Bangladesh – through the **MCH-FP programme (DGFP/DGHS, MNCAH Operational Plan)**.

---

#### Q.42

**Question:**
Mention the MCH problems of Developing and Developed Countries.

**Answer:**
**Developing countries (e.g., Bangladesh):**
1. High **maternal mortality and morbidity** (haemorrhage, eclampsia, obstructed labour, sepsis, unsafe abortion), **anaemia** and malnutrition in women, early marriage/teenage pregnancy, high fertility, short birth interval.
2. **High perinatal, neonatal, infant and under-five mortality** – birth asphyxia, prematurity/LBW, neonatal sepsis, diarrhoea, ARI, malnutrition, measles and other vaccine-preventable diseases.
3. **Low birth weight**, protein-energy malnutrition, vitamin A and iron deficiency, poor breastfeeding/weaning practices.
4. **Inadequate antenatal care, non-institutional/ unskilled deliveries**, poor postnatal care.
5. **Infectious diseases** (malaria, TB, HIV), poor environmental sanitation, illiteracy, poverty, low status of women, gender discrimination.
6. Limited access to health services, shortage of skilled staff and emergency obstetric care.

**Developed countries:**
1. **Low mortality, but problems of prematurity, congenital malformations, genetic disorders, perinatal conditions** and sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS).
2. **Accidents/injuries, child abuse and neglect, drug/alcohol abuse, smoking** in pregnancy.
3. **Teenage and unmarried pregnancy**, social and emotional/behavioural problems, mental health problems, obesity, learning disabilities.
4. **Advanced maternal age/assisted reproduction complications**, multiple pregnancies; lifestyle disease (obesity, diabetes) and chronic diseases (asthma, allergies, childhood cancers), childhood disability.

#### Q.43

**Question:**
MMR has been decreased in Bangladesh – How?

**Answer:**
**Trend:** maternal mortality ratio fell from about **574 (1986–90) to ≈ 196 (2016) and ≈ 120–123 per 100,000 live births (2022–2023 estimates; ≈ 115 in UN estimates 2023)** – a reduction of nearly 80% since 2000 (SDG 2030 target: 70).

**Reasons for the decline:**
1. **Declining fertility** (TFR from ~6 in the 1970s to 2.3) → fewer high-risk pregnancies; wider contraception coverage.
2. **Improved coverage of maternal health services:** antenatal care (4+ visits ≈ 41% in 2022 vs 17% in 2004), **skilled birth attendance ≈ 70%**, **facility-based delivery ≈ 65%**, **postnatal care within 48 hours ≈ 55%** (BDHS 2022).
3. **Comprehensive and basic emergency obstetric care (EmOC)** at upazila and district facilities, referral systems, 24/7 delivery services, caesarean-section availability.
4. **Trained human resources** – community skilled birth attendants (CSBAs), **professional midwives** (3-year diploma), nurses, doctors; life-saving training.
5. **Demand-side financing – Maternal Health Voucher Scheme**, free services at public facilities, **maternal and neonatal care at community clinics**.
6. **Interventions:** misoprostol/oxytocin for prevention of postpartum haemorrhage, magnesium sulphate for eclampsia, **Menstrual regulation** services reducing unsafe abortion, iron-folic acid supplementation, tetanus immunisation, clean delivery kits.
7. **Maternal death surveillance and response (MDSR)** and Maternal and Perinatal Death Review; improved quality of care.
8. **Socio-economic improvements** – female education, women's empowerment, improved roads/communication, ambulance services, mobile health, NGO involvement (BRAC, icddr,b), national strategy for maternal health.
*Challenges remain: ≈ one third of births at home, regional/urban–rural inequity, over-medicalisation (high caesarean rate).*

#### Q.45

**Question:**
Define Milk Injury. What are the causes of LBW?

**Answer:**
**Milk injury:** the **ill-effects/hazards resulting from improper artificial (bottle/formula or animal milk) feeding of infants** – i.e., disease and malnutrition due to wrong or contaminated milk substitutes (over-dilution → malnutrition; contamination → diarrhoea/infection; allergy; cow's-milk anaemia; hypernatraemia; obesity). It underlines the superiority of breastfeeding.

**Low birth weight (LBW):** birth weight **<2,500 g** (up to and including 2,499 g) irrespective of gestational age.

**Causes of LBW:**
1. **Maternal factors:** small maternal stature, **poor nutrition/low pre-pregnancy weight/inadequate weight gain**, **anaemia**, teenage (<18/<20 years) or advanced age (>35), **short birth interval, high parity**, previous LBW, chronic diseases (hypertension/pre-eclampsia, diabetes, heart/kidney disease), infections (malaria, syphilis, UTI, TORCH), **smoking/tobacco, alcohol**, heavy physical work, low socio-economic status, low education.
2. **Foetal factors:** **multiple pregnancy**, congenital anomalies, chromosomal disorders, intrauterine infections, foetal growth restriction.
3. **Placental and uterine factors:** placental insufficiency, placenta previa, abruptio placentae, uterine malformations, cervical incompetence.
4. **Environmental/other:** pollution, arsenic and other toxins, high altitude, lack of antenatal care, stress, unsafe/ unplanned pregnancy.
LBW is both **prematurity (preterm)** and **small-for-gestational-age (IUGR)**; in Bangladesh, LBW remains a leading risk factor for neonatal death.

#### Q.46

**Question:**
Mention the Advantages of Breastfeeding.

**Answer:**
**Advantages:**
**For the infant:**
1. **Nutritionally ideal** – perfectly balanced protein (whey/lactalbumin), fat (essential fatty acids), lactose, vitamins, minerals; easily digested; always at proper temperature; **colostrum** rich in immunoglobulins and vitamin A.
2. **Anti-infective/immunological** – secretory IgA, lactoferrin, lysozyme, bifidus factor, leukocytes → protects against diarrhoea, ARI, otitis media, NEC; sepsis.
3. **Safe/hygienic and free from contamination;** no bottle-feeding hazards (milk injury).
4. Promotes **growth and brain development** (DHA), reduces risk of obesity, diabetes, allergy/eczema, SIDS and some childhood cancers; better IQ.
5. **Psychological** – mother–baby bonding, emotional security.
**For the mother:**
6. **Natural contraceptive effect** (lactational amenorrhoea – up to 6 months if exclusive).
7. **Faster uterine involution, less postpartum haemorrhage**, helps return to pre-pregnancy weight; reduces **breast and ovarian cancer** risk.
**Economic and social:**
8. **Economical** (no cost, no fuel/utensils) – saves family and national resources; no preparation time.
*Bangladesh:* WHO/UNICEF – **early initiation within 1 hour, exclusive breastfeeding for 6 months, continue up to 2 years**; the Breast-milk Substitutes (Marketing) Act 2013 regulates formula promotion; maternity leave of 6 months for public servants (and 16 weeks under Labour Act) supports breastfeeding.

**Question:**
Define Weaning. Name some Weaning Foods. What is Milk Injury?

**Weaning:** the **process of gradually introducing the infant to foods other than breast milk (complementary feeding) while breastfeeding continues, ending with complete cessation of breastfeeding**; semi-solid weaning foods are started **at 6 completed months**.

- Commercial weaning mixes (e.g., Cerelac, Farex) – not necessary/costly.

**Milk injury:** the hazards of improper artificial feeding of an infant – **bottle-feeding, over-diluted formula or animal milk** causing malnutrition/marasmus, diarrhoea and infections (contaminated bottles/teats), allergy, anaemia and obesity (see Q.45).

#### Q.48

**Question:**
Define Weaning. Name some Weaning Foods. Mention the criteria of Weaning Foods?

**Answer:**
**Weaning:** the gradual process of **introducing complementary (semi-solid/solid) foods to the infant, from about 6 months of age, along with continued breastfeeding, until the child completely stops breast milk**.

**Weaning foods (examples):** khichuri, mashed rice with dal/egg/fish/vegetables, suji or rice porridge, mashed banana/papaya, egg yolk, mashed potato/pumpkin, fish/chicken/liver, fruit and vegetable purees, milk preparations (kheer/ custard).

**Criteria (characteristics) of a good weaning food:**
1. **Rich in energy and protein** and adequately supplied with vitamins and minerals (**energy dense – with oil/ghee, protein from pulses, egg, fish, meat**).
2. **Culturally acceptable**, palatable, and prepared from **locally available, low-cost foods**.
3. **Soft, semi-solid consistency, easy to swallow and digest**; low bulk (low viscosity); **free from lumps/irritants and spices**; **low in fibre**.
4. **Hygienically prepared and stored** – freshly made, using clean water and utensils (cup and spoon, not bottle) – free from contamination.
5. **Introduced gradually** (one new food at a time), appropriate for the age, with increasing quantity/frequency (2–3 times/day at 6–8 months; 3–4 times at 9–23 months + snacks) and diversity (≥ 5 of 8 food groups).
6. Prepared **at home**; not a replacement for breast milk; **free from adding sugar/salt**; encourages responsive feeding.

### Family Planning (FP)

#### Q.50

**Question:**
Define Eligible Couple and Target Couple. Mention their Importance. Classify Contraceptive Methods.

**Answer:**
**Eligible couple:** a **currently married couple in which the wife is in the reproductive age group (15–45 years; 15–49 in BDHS/Bangladesh programme)**. **Target couple:** an **eligible couple with 2 or 3 living children** (especially wife <35 years) who are the specific targets of family-planning motivation for **limiting/spacing** births.

**Importance:**
- Eligible couples are the **population served by the programme** – used for **estimating contraceptive needs**, planning services, and calculating **couple protection rate/CPR**; maintained in the **eligible couple register** by FWA/Family Welfare Assistants.
- Target couples are the priority for **terminal/long-acting methods** (more chance of reducing fertility); help to set targets; monitoring and evaluation.

**Classification of contraceptive methods:**
1. **Natural/traditional:** periodic abstinence (calendar, basal body temperature, cervical mucus), withdrawal (coitus interruptus), **lactational amenorrhoea method (LAM)**.
2. **Barrier methods:** male condom, female condom, diaphragm, cervical cap, **spermicides** (foam, jelly, tablets).
3. **Intrauterine devices (IUD):** non-medicated, copper (Cu T), hormone-releasing (LNG-IUS).
4. **Hormonal methods:** combined oral contraceptive pills (OCP), progestogen-only pills (POP/minipill), injectables (DMPA, NET-EN), implants (Implanon/Jadelle), emergency contraception, vaginal ring/patch.
5. **Surgical (terminal/permanent) methods:** **vasectomy** (including no-scalpel vasectomy – NSV), **tubectomy** (female sterilisation – minilap/laparoscopic).
6. **Post-conceptional methods:** menstrual regulation/MTP (up to 12 weeks) – *in Bangladesh, MR is permitted by policy and part of the programme*.
*Bangladesh:* DGFP provides services through FWAs, union health & family welfare centres, UHCs; contraceptive prevalence has plateaued at about 62–64% since 2011, with pills, injectables and condoms the main methods and unmet need still important.

#### Q.51

**Question:**
Classify IUCDs. Mention mode of action of Cu T.

**Answer:**
**Classification of IUCDs (by generation):**
1. **First generation – non-medicated (inert):** Lippes loop, Margulies spiral, Saf-T-coil.
2. **Second generation – copper devices:** Cu T 200, **Cu T 380A**, Multiload (Cu 250, Cu 375), Nova T.
3. **Third generation – hormone-releasing devices:** progesterone-releasing (Progestasert), **levonorgestrel-releasing (LNG-IUS, Mirena)**.

**Mode of action of Cu T:**
1. Copper ions released are **toxic to sperm and ovum** – impair sperm motility and viability → **prevent fertilisation**.
2. **Sterile inflammatory (foreign body) reaction in the endometrium** – leukocytes/macrophages/ enzymes hostile to sperm, ovum and blastocyst.
3. Alters the uterine and tubal fluid and **endometrium (biochemical changes), preventing implantation** (rarely, as a secondary effect).
4. Interferes with sperm transport through the cervix/uterus/tubes.
*Cu T 380A is effective for 10 years (Bangladesh programme standard method for long-acting reversible contraception).*

#### Q.52 (CASE)

**Question:**
A motivated eligible female came to insert IUCD. What type of IUCD would be chosen and what advice would be given for that lady?

**Answer:**
**Type chosen:** **Copper T 380A** (second generation) – long-acting (10 years), effective (failure <1%), reversible, no hormones; it is the IUCD supplied in the national programme. (LNG-IUS if heavy menstrual bleeding/dysmenorrhoea is the main concern.)

**Pre-insertion counselling and assessment:** history/examination (pelvic examination, rule out pregnancy, PID/STI, anaemia), informed consent, choice of method, explain risks.
**Timing:** within **first 7–12 days of menstrual cycle** (when pregnancy is unlikely – also anytime if pregnancy can be ruled out); **post-partum** – within 48 hours (post-placental) or after 4–6 weeks; immediately **post-abortion/menstrual regulation**.

**Post-insertion advice:**
1. Explain expected effects: cramps, spotting, **increased menstrual bleeding/pain in first 2–3 months** – usually settle.
2. **Check the thread (string) after each period**; do not remove by herself.
3. **Follow-up visit at 4–6 weeks** (after first/second period) and yearly; return anytime for problems.
4. **Danger signs – return at once:** missed period/late period (pregnancy), abdominal pain, abnormal discharge/fever (infection), heavy bleeding, thread missing or longer/ device felt.
5. Use **condoms** if at risk of STI (IUCD does not protect from STIs/HIV).
6. Removal date (Cu T 380A at 10 years); can be removed any time on request; fertility returns rapidly.

**Question:**
Mention the Complications and Contraindications of IUDs.

**Answer:**
**Complications:**
- **Immediate:** pain/cramps, vasovagal syncope, **uterine perforation** (rare), bleeding.
- **Early/late:** **menorrhagia/intermenstrual bleeding, dysmenorrhoea (commonest reasons for removal)**, **expulsion** (partial/complete), **pelvic inflammatory disease**, pregnancy with IUD in situ (increased risk of ectopic pregnancy, abortion, preterm labour/sepsis), missing thread, embedding/perforation, anaemia, leucorrhoea.

**Contraindications:**
- **Absolute:** pregnancy, **active pelvic infection/PID or puerperal/post-abortal sepsis**, genital tract malignancy (cervical/endometrial), **unexplained vaginal bleeding**, distorted uterine cavity (congenital anomalies/large fibroids), **current STI/ cervicitis**, Wilson's disease or copper allergy (Cu IUD).
- **Relative:** previous ectopic pregnancy, valvular heart disease, immunosuppression, severe dysmenorrhoea/menorrhagia, anaemia, nulliparity with high STI risk, previous pelvic surgery, history of expulsion.

Who are the Ideal candidates of IUDs?

Ideal candidates for IUD:
1. **Women who have had at least one child** (parous), wanting **spacing** or long-term reversible contraception.
2. **Women with no contraindications** (no PID/STI risk, normal uterine cavity).
3. Women in a **stable monogamous relationship** (low risk of STI).

#### Q.55

**Question:**
What is PoP? Mention other names of POP.

**Answer:**
**POP = Progestogen-only pill** – an oral contraceptive containing **only a low dose of a progestogen (e.g., levonorgestrel 30 µg, norethisterone 350 µg, desogestrel 75 µg) and no oestrogen**, taken **daily without a pill-free break**, at the same time of the day (within 3 hours; desogestrel 12 hours).
**Mode of action:** thickens cervical mucus (blocks sperm), thin atrophic endometrium, reduces tubal motility; inhibits ovulation (desogestrel type, partly in others).
**Other names:** **Minipill**, **progestin-only pill (POP/POC)**, **progestogen-only oral contraceptive**, "micropill"; sometimes called the **breast-feeding (lactation) pill**.

#### Q.56

**Question:**
Mention the advantages and disadvantages of Pop over OCP.

**Answer:**
**Advantages of POP over combined OCP:**
1. **No oestrogen-related complications** – no increased risk of thromboembolism, stroke, hypertension.
2. **Safe in lactation** – does not affect (may increase) milk quantity/quality; can be started after 6 weeks postpartum.
3. Suitable for women **over 35 years, smokers, hypertensive, diabetics, with migraine or those with contraindications to oestrogen**.
4. Fewer side effects such as nausea, headache, breast tenderness; can reduce dysmenorrhoea; rapid return of fertility.
5. May be used throughout lactation as a good contraceptive (additive to LAM).

**Disadvantages of POP compared to OCP:**
1. **Menstrual irregularities** (irregular bleeding/spotting, amenorrhoea) – commonest cause of discontinuation; **does not regulate cycle**.
2. **Slightly lower effectiveness (higher failure rate) especially in non-lactating women**, and **must be taken at the same time daily** (missing >3 hours reduces efficacy) – needs strict compliance.
3. Higher risk of **ectopic pregnancy if failure occurs**; functional ovarian cysts.
4. No pill-free interval (taken continuously); no protection from STIs; fewer non-contraceptive benefits (e.g., less protection from ovarian/endometrial cancer, acne control).

#### Q.57

**Question:**
Mention the Complications of Vasectomy. What Post-Operative advice will you give after Vasectomy.

**Answer:**
**Complications of vasectomy:**
- **Immediate/early:** bleeding, scrotal haematoma/ecchymosis, wound infection, pain/swelling, vasovagal reaction.
- **Late:** **sperm granuloma**, epididymo-orchitis/epididymal congestion, **chronic scrotal pain (post-vasectomy pain syndrome)**, **recanalisation → failure (pregnancy)**, psychological problems (impotence/regret) – *vasectomy does not affect libido, erection, ejaculation, hormone levels*; antisperm antibodies, reversal difficulty.

**Post-operative advice:**
1. **Rest** for 24–48 hours; avoid heavy lifting/strenuous activities (cycling, long travel) for about a week.
2. **Keep the wound clean and dry** (no bath/immersion for 2–3 days); wear **tight underpants/scrotal support**.
3. **Analgesics** (paracetamol) and **ice pack** for pain/swelling; complete antibiotics if prescribed.
4. **Sexual activity** can be resumed after a few days when comfortable, **but use another contraceptive (condoms) until sterility is confirmed** – i.e., for **3 months or about 20 ejaculations** (semen analysis for azoospermia).
5. **Return immediately** if severe pain/swelling, bleeding, pus/fever; follow-up at 1 week and semen testing at 3 months.
6. Reassure: no effect on sexual performance and masculinity; the sterilisation is **permanent** (reversal not guaranteed).
*Bangladesh: no-scalpel vasectomy (NSV) is offered free; male sterilisation prevalence is low (<1%).*

#### Q.58

**Question:**
When a Male and a Female will become sterile after Tubectomy and Vasectomy respectively?

**Answer:**
- **Female after tubectomy:** **immediately** after the procedure (tubes are occluded and the ovum cannot meet sperm); effective at once (conceptions only through failure – recanalisation/ pre-existing pregnancy).
- **Male after vasectomy:** **not immediately** – viable sperm remain in the vas deferens/seminal vesicles beyond the site of ligation; sterility is achieved after about **3 months or ~20 ejaculations** (when semen analysis shows **azoospermia/no motile sperm**). Another contraceptive (condom) must be used until then.

*(Remember: "Tubectomy – female sterilisation, immediate; Vasectomy – male sterilisation, delayed.")*

### Demography

#### Q.59

**Question:**
Define Demography. Name the Demographic Processes. Mention the stages for Demographic Cycle.

**Answer:**
**Demography:** the scientific study of **human populations – their size, composition (structure), distribution and changes – and the factors responsible for those changes** (fertility, mortality, migration).

**Demographic processes:**
1. **Fertility** (births)
2. **Mortality** (deaths)
3. **Marriage**
4. **Migration**
5. **Social mobility**
*(The three that directly change population size are fertility, mortality and migration.)*

**Stages of the demographic cycle:**
1. **High stationary** – high birth rate, high death rate (stable/slow growth).
2. **Early expanding** – birth rate remains high, death rate begins to fall → rapid growth.
3. **Late expanding** – birth rate begins to decline, death rate low → growth slows.
4. **Low stationary** – low birth rate and low death rate (stable).
5. **Declining** – birth rate falls below death rate → population decreases.

*Bangladesh is in the **late expanding** stage, approaching low-stationary (TFR 2.3, CBR ≈ 20, CDR ≈ 5).*

#### Q.60

**Question:**
In a Census the population was counted according to Usual Site of Residence. What type of Census it was? Mention the Uses of Census.

**Answer:**
**Type: De jure census** (population counted by **usual place of residence**, wherever they are at the time of census). In contrast, a **de facto census** counts people **where they are found on the census night** (actual presence). Bangladesh's 6th Population and Housing Census (June 2022, BBS) was the country's first digital census.

**Uses of census:**
1. **Population size, distribution and growth** (birth rate, death rate, growth rate, density).
2. **Population composition** – age, sex, marital status, literacy, occupation, religion, urban–rural residence.
3. **Planning and administration** – health, education, housing, employment, transport; allocation of resources, constituency delimitation.
4. **Denominator for health indicators** (rates, ratios; e.g., IMR, MMR, vaccination coverage) and for estimating needs (e.g., eligible couples, school-age children, ageing population).
5. **Evaluation of programmes**, projections and demographic research, monitoring SDGs.
6. Basis for sample frames for national surveys (BDHS, MICS, etc.), industry/commerce/ market decisions.
*Bangladesh Census 2022 (final): population ≈ **169.8 million**, growth rate **1.22%**, density **1,119/km²**, literacy 74.8%, females outnumber males for the first time.*

#### Q.61

**Question:**
How Growth rate is calculated? Mention the current Growth Rate in Bangladesh? How …

**Answer:**
**Calculation of population growth rate:**
- **Natural increase** = Births − Deaths. **Crude rate of natural increase = CBR − CDR** (per 1,000).
- **Annual growth rate (%) = (CBR − CDR) ÷ 10** (± net migration rate); or from census: **Growth rate = [(P₂ − P₁) ÷ P₁] × 100 ÷ n** (years) (compound/exponential growth formula: r = ln(P₂/P₁)/n).

**Current growth rate in Bangladesh:** **1.22% per annum (Census 2022; earlier 1.37% in 2011)**.

**Measures to reduce the growth rate:** effective family planning services, delaying age at marriage, female education and empowerment, reducing infant/child mortality, economic development, and community motivation.

---

#### Q.62

**Question:**
Doubling Time is calculated?

**Answer:**
**Doubling time** = the number of years required for a population to double in size at the current growth rate.

**Formula:** **Doubling time (years) = 70 ÷ annual growth rate (%)** (the "rule of 70", from 0.693/r).

**Example for Bangladesh:** growth rate 1.22% → 70 ÷ 1.22 ≈ **57 years** (i.e., population would double in ≈ 57 years if the present growth continued; earlier at 2.5% it was 28 years).

#### Q.63

**Question:**
What is NRR-1. When NRR I would be achieved?

**Answer:**
**Net reproduction rate (NRR)** = the number of daughters a newborn girl will bear during her lifetime, assuming prevailing age-specific fertility and mortality rates (i.e., fertility *adjusted for mortality of women until the end of reproductive age*).
**NRR = 1 (NRR-1)** means each generation of mothers is **exactly replaced by a generation of daughters** – **replacement-level fertility**, i.e., **TFR ≈ 2.1** (where a woman has on average ~2.1 children, 1 daughter), at which the population would eventually become **stationary** (zero growth, after population momentum is exhausted – 50–60 years). NRR < 1 → population declines; NRR > 1 → population grows.

**When achieved:** when the TFR reaches replacement level (~2.1). In **Bangladesh, TFR has stalled at ≈ 2.3 (BDHS 2011–2022)**, so NRR is still slightly above 1 (about 1.1). The national targets (National Population Policy, HPNSP) of attaining NRR-1 (TFR 2.1 or lower) were not met on time; it is expected within the next few years if the contraceptive prevalence rate rises, unmet need falls and age at marriage rises. Even after NRR-1 is reached, population growth continues for decades because of **population momentum** (young age structure).

#### Q.66

**Question:**
What are the criteria of ideal class room?

**Answer:**
Criteria of an ideal classroom:
1. **Location/site:** quiet, away from noise (roads, markets), well-drained, with playground; safe from floods; safe access.
2. **Space:** adequate – about **1 sq metre (10 sq ft) floor area per pupil** and **adequate air space**; not more than **~40 pupils per class** (in practice, lower the better).
3. **Lighting:** adequate natural light – windows with total area of **at least one-fifth (20–25%) of floor area**; light from the left/ left and back (avoids shadows); glare avoided; artificial lighting (≥ 200–300 lux) when needed.
4. **Ventilation:** cross-ventilation with windows/doors on opposite sides; comfortable temperature (below ~ 30°C), good air movement.
5. **Seating:** desks and benches of **appropriate size/height for age**, adjustable, giving correct posture (to prevent kyphosis/scoliosis, eye strain), arranged for a clear view of the blackboard.
6. **Blackboard:** non-glossy, matt black/green, positioned at proper height and lighting, with chalk dust control.
7. **Walls and ceiling:** light colours (cream/pale), smooth, washable; ceilings white; floor easy to clean.

#### Q.68

**Question:**
Mention the criteria of a midday school meal? Mention the importance of midday school meal service.

**Answer:**
**Criteria of a mid-day school meal:**
1. Provides about **1/3 of total energy and 1/2 of protein** requirements of the child.

*Bangladesh:* School Feeding Programme (fortified biscuits, now expanding to cooked meals under the National School Meal Policy 2019) improved enrolment and attendance in primary schools.

## 6. Occupational Health

#### Q.70

**Question:**
Define Occupational Health. State the objectives of Occupational Health.

**Answer:**
**Definition (ILO/WHO Joint Committee):** Occupational health is the **promotion and maintenance of the highest degree of physical, mental and social well-being of workers in all occupations**, by **preventing departures from health, controlling risks, and adapting work to people and each person to his/her job**.

**Objectives:**
1. **Promotion and maintenance of the highest degree of physical, mental and social well-being** of workers in all occupations.
2. **Prevention of departures from health** among workers caused by their working conditions (occupational diseases, work-related diseases, accidents).
3. **Protection of workers** in their employment from risks resulting from factors adverse to health.
4. **Placing and maintaining the worker in an occupational environment adapted to his physiological and psychological capabilities** (right man in the right job).
5. **Adaptation of work to man and of each man to his job.**

*Bangladesh:* Bangladesh Labour Act 2006 (amended 2013, 2018) and Bangladesh Labour Rules 2015; Department of Inspection for Factories and Establishments (DIFE) under the Ministry of Labour and Employment; National Occupational Safety and Health Policy 2013. Major events that shaped OSH reform: Tazreen Factory fire (2012) and Rana Plaza collapse (2013), followed by the Accord/Alliance and the National Tripartite Plan of Action.

---

#### Q.71

**Question:**
Define occupational environment. Mention types of interaction in a working environment.

**Answer:**
**Occupational (working) environment:** the sum total of **all external conditions and influences (physical, chemical, biological, mechanical, ergonomic and psychosocial) that surround the worker at the workplace** and that affect his/her health, safety, comfort and efficiency.

**Types of interaction in a working environment:**
1. **Man and the physical/chemical/biological environment** – exposure to heat, light, noise, dust, fumes, gases, chemicals, microorganisms.
2. **Man and machine** – tools, equipment, machinery (mechanical injuries, vibration, noise, posture and operating strain).
3. **Man and his work/job** – task content, work load, work posture, repetition, shift and working hours (fatigue, musculoskeletal strain).
4. **Man and other people (psychosocial)** – relationships with co-workers, supervisors and management; job satisfaction, stress, harassment.

---

#### Q.72

**Question:**
Define Ergonomics. Mention the objectives of Ergonomics.

**Answer:**
**Ergonomics:** the **scientific study of the relationship between man and his working environment** – the application of human biological sciences together with engineering to achieve **optimum mutual adjustment of man and his work**, the benefit being measured in terms of human efficiency and well-being; in short, **"fitting the job to the worker"** (Greek *ergon* = work, *nomos* = law).

**Objectives:**
1. To **adapt the job, machines, tools and workplace to the physical and mental capacities of the worker**.
2. To **increase efficiency and productivity** of the worker.
3. To **reduce fatigue, discomfort, strain and musculoskeletal disorders**.
4. To **prevent accidents and occupational diseases**, ensuring safety.
5. To **improve job satisfaction, comfort and well-being** of the worker.
6. To determine proper design of work posture, work-rest schedule, lighting, seating and control layout.

#### Q.78

**Question:**
Define pneumoconiosis. State the factors causing pneumoconiosis.

**Answer:**
**Pneumoconiosis:** a group of **occupational lung diseases caused by the inhalation of dust (mineral or organic) and the permanent tissue (fibrotic) reaction of the lungs to it**; it is irreversible, progressive and often incapacitating. Examples: silicosis, anthracosis (coal worker's pneumoconiosis), asbestosis, siderosis, berylliosis, byssinosis, bagassosis.

**Factors responsible for causing/influencing pneumoconiosis:**
1. **Nature (chemical composition) of the dust** – e.g., free silica content, fibrogenic dusts (silica, asbestos) are more harmful than inert dusts.
2. **Concentration of dust** in the air breathed (dust load).
3. **Size of dust particles** – particles **0.5–5 µm (respirable)** reach alveoli and are retained; larger particles are filtered out in the upper airways.
4. **Shape and solubility of particles** – needle/fibre-shaped (asbestos) particles more harmful; soluble dusts less so.
5. **Duration of exposure** (years of exposure and hours per day).
6. **Individual susceptibility** – age, smoking, pre-existing lung disease (especially tuberculosis, chronic bronchitis), nutritional and immune status.
7. **Working conditions** – poor ventilation, lack of dust control, absence of respiratory protection, heavy physical work (increased ventilation).

#### Q.79 (CASE)

**Question:**
You are an Industrial Medical Officer. A person who has been working in a Sugar Mill for 25 years has come to you with the complaints of breathlessness, cough, hemoptysis and slight fever. As a medical officer what will you suspect and what investigations will you advise? (Case-based question)

**Answer:**
**Suspected diagnosis: Bagassosis** – an occupational (hypersensitivity) lung disease from inhalation of **bagasse (dried sugarcane fibre) dust**, contaminated with actinomycete spores (*Thermoactinomyces sacchari*). It presents with cough, breathlessness, haemoptysis and low-grade fever, and chronic exposure causes diffuse pulmonary fibrosis. **Differential diagnoses to exclude:** pulmonary tuberculosis, other pneumoconioses/hypersensitivity pneumonitis (farmer's lung), bronchiectasis, lung cancer.

**Investigations:**
1. **Detailed occupational history** (job in the mill, exposure to bagasse, duration, use of protective measures) and examination.
2. **Chest X-ray** – diffuse fine mottling/nodular or reticulonodular shadows, later fibrosis.
3. **High-resolution CT chest** – ground-glass opacities, centrilobular nodules, fibrosis.
4. **Lung function tests (spirometry, DLCO)** – restrictive pattern with reduced diffusing capacity.
5. **Serology – precipitin test (serum precipitating antibodies) against *Thermoactinomyces sacchari*/bagasse extract.**
6. **Sputum examination** – smear for AFB/Xpert MTB/RIF (to exclude TB), culture.
7. **Blood tests** – complete blood count (eosinophilia), ESR.
8. **Bronchoalveolar lavage** (lymphocytosis) and **lung biopsy** if diagnosis is uncertain.

**Management and prevention:** remove from further exposure (change of job), corticosteroids, treat complications; **prevention** – dust control by wet methods/ventilation, **spraying bagasse with 2% propionic acid** (prevents mould growth), respirators/masks, use of bagasse in closed processes, **pre-placement and periodic medical examination** and health education.
