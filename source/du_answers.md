# Model answers — DU written bank
Assumed 5 marks unless marks are stated in the question.

## DU.1
**Definition**

**Community medicine** is the branch of medicine that studies health and disease in the population of a defined community or group. Its goal is to find the health problems and needs of that population (**community diagnosis**) and to plan, carry out and evaluate health measures that meet those needs (**community treatment**) (Faculty of Community Medicine, Royal Colleges of Physicians, UK, 1972).

**Evolution**

Hygiene → Public health → Preventive medicine → Social medicine → **Community medicine** (1970s)

**Key features**

1. Unit of concern: the **community**, not the single patient.
2. Emphasis on **promotion and prevention**, integrated with curative and rehabilitative care (**comprehensive health care**).
3. Basic tools: **epidemiology** and **biostatistics**.
4. Active approach: reaches the whole population, healthy and sick.
5. Priority to **vulnerable groups**: mothers, children, elderly, poor, workers.
6. Delivered by a **health team** with **community participation** and **intersectoral coordination**.
7. Built on **primary health care** to achieve **Health for All**.

**Scope**

Epidemiology; biostatistics and research; control of communicable and non-communicable diseases; nutrition; environmental and occupational health; MCH, family planning and demography; health education; health planning and management; national and international health; social and behavioural sciences.

**Related terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Preventive medicine | Prevention of disease in individuals and groups by specific measures |
| Social medicine | Study of man as a social being in his total environment |
| Community health | Health status of a community and organised action to improve it |

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 1.

## DU.2
**Aim**

Highest attainable level of health for the whole community (**Health for All**), with equity, through promotive, preventive, curative and rehabilitative services.

**Objectives**

1. **Health promotion**: health education, nutrition, healthy lifestyle, sanitation.
2. **Prevention of disease**: specific protection (immunisation, chemoprophylaxis); primordial to tertiary levels.
3. **Early diagnosis and treatment**: screening, case finding, surveillance.
4. **Disability limitation and rehabilitation**.
5. **Community diagnosis**: size, distribution and determinants of health problems (epidemiology, biostatistics).
6. **Community treatment**: plan, implement and evaluate health services and national programmes.
7. **Care of vulnerable groups**: mothers, under-fives, school children, adolescents, workers, elderly, disabled.
8. **Control of communicable and non-communicable diseases**; epidemic and disaster management.
9. **Healthy environment**: safe water, sanitation, housing, safe workplaces.
10. **Health management**: efficient use of resources, intersectoral coordination, community participation.
11. **Research**: causes, risk factors, effective and affordable interventions.
12. **Training** of a community-oriented health team.

**Three objectives (short answer)**

1. Promote health and prevent disease in the community.
2. Identify community health problems and needs (**community diagnosis**).
3. Plan, implement and evaluate health measures to meet those needs (**community treatment**).

**Educational objective (MBBS)**

Produce a **community-oriented doctor** who can diagnose community problems, run national programmes and lead the health team at primary level (Upazila and below).

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 1.

## DU.3
**Differences between community medicine and clinical medicine**

| Point | Community medicine | Clinical medicine |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of concern | Community / population group | Individual patient |
| Main focus | Health promotion and disease prevention | Diagnosis and cure of disease |
| Approach | Active: seeks out healthy and sick | Passive: waits for the patient |
| Diagnosis | **Community diagnosis** | **Clinical diagnosis** |
| Basis of diagnosis | Rates, ratios, surveys, surveillance data | History, examination, laboratory tests |
| Tools | Epidemiology, biostatistics, social sciences | Clinical methods, diagnostic tests |
| Treatment | **Community treatment**: health programmes, health education, environmental measures | Drugs, surgery, nursing care |
| Evaluation | Change in health indicators (incidence, IMR, coverage) | Cure, recovery, relief of symptoms |
| Concept of health | Holistic, ecological | Mainly biomedical |
| Setting | Home, school, workplace, field, health centres | Hospital, clinic |
| Personnel | Health team, community, other sectors | Doctor, nurse, specialists |
| Responsibility | Whole population, including the unaffected | Patients under care |
| Example | Measles vaccination campaign lowers incidence | Treatment of measles pneumonia in hospital |

**Relationship**

The two are **complementary**: clinical records feed community diagnosis; community medicine sets priorities and preventive measures that cut the clinical load.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 1.

## DU.4
**A. WHO "Five-star doctor" (C. Boelen, WHO)**

| Role | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1. Care provider | High-quality, continuous, comprehensive care of the patient as a whole person in family and community |
| 2. Decision-maker | Chooses evidence-based, cost-effective technologies and treatments |
| 3. Communicator | Promotes healthy lifestyles by explanation and advice; empowers people |
| 4. Community leader | Wins community trust; balances individual and community needs; starts action |
| 5. Manager | Works with the health team and other sectors; uses health data for decisions |

**B. Other roles**

1. **Health educator and promoter**: counselling on diet, tobacco, family planning, hygiene.
2. **Preventive role**: immunisation, ANC, screening, early detection.
3. **Public health role**: surveillance, **notification** of diseases, outbreak investigation, disaster response.
4. **Programme implementer**: EPI, TB, malaria, family planning, NCD control.
5. **Team leader and trainer**: supervises nurses, health assistants, CHCPs.
6. **Researcher**: collects data, evaluates services, practises evidence-based medicine.
7. **Medicolegal role**: birth, death and fitness certificates; injury reports; court evidence.
8. **Advocate**: for the poor and vulnerable and for healthy public policy.
9. **Ethical role**: confidentiality, informed consent, equity, rational prescribing.
10. **Social role**: counsellor, role model, link between people and the health system; intersectoral coordination.

**C. In Bangladesh**

Doctors at Upazila Health Complexes provide first-referral care, supervise community clinics and run national programmes at field level.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 1; WHO, The five-star doctor (Boelen C).

## DU.5
**A. Professional qualities**

1. Sound, up-to-date **knowledge**; lifelong learning (CME/CPD).
2. Clinical **competence**: history, examination, diagnosis, procedures.
3. Sound **judgement**; evidence-based, rational decisions.
4. Good **communication**: listens, explains in the patient's language, breaks bad news gently.
5. Record keeping, time management, teamwork, leadership.

**B. Personal qualities**

1. **Compassion and empathy**; kindness, patience, courtesy.
2. **Integrity and honesty**; humility; knows own limits and refers.
3. Punctuality, discipline, neat appearance, emotional stability.
4. Physical and mental fitness; self-care.

**C. Ethical qualities (four principles)**

1. **Autonomy**: informed consent; respect for choices.
2. **Beneficence**: acts in the patient's best interest.
3. **Non-maleficence**: first, do no harm.
4. **Justice**: equal care regardless of wealth, religion, sex or politics.
Plus **confidentiality**, truthfulness, no exploitation, no conflict of interest.

**D. Social / community qualities**

1. Preventive outlook; health educator.
2. **Social accountability**; serves the poor and rural people.
3. Respect for local culture and beliefs.

**Deeper insight**

- Treats the **person, not just the disease** (holistic, psychosocial).
- Combines science (competence), art (empathy) and ethics (conscience).
- Is a **five-star doctor** (WHO): care provider, decision-maker, communicator, community leader, manager.
- Follows professional codes: Hippocratic Oath, WMA Declaration of Geneva (Physician's Pledge, 2017), BMDC Code of Professional Conduct, Etiquette and Ethics.
- Earns and keeps **public trust**, the basis of the doctor–patient relationship.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 1; WMA Declaration of Geneva, 2017.

## DU.6
**Integrity**

- **Definition**: steady adherence to moral and professional principles: honest, truthful and fair in every act, whether observed or not.
- In medical practice:
  1. Honest history, notes, reports and certificates; no false certificates.
  2. Truthful disclosure of diagnosis, prognosis and **errors**.
  3. No fee-splitting, kickbacks or gifts from drug companies; declare **conflicts of interest**.
  4. Rational prescribing and investigations; no unnecessary procedures.
  5. Research integrity: no fabrication, falsification or plagiarism.
  6. Respect for confidentiality and consent.
  7. Work within competence; refer when needed.

**Accountability**

- **Definition**: the duty of a doctor to answer for professional decisions and actions, accept responsibility for their outcome, and be open to review and sanction.
- Accountable to:

| To whom | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Patient and family | Informed consent, explanation, complaint handling |
| Profession | BMDC registration, code of conduct, disciplinary action, peer review |
| Employer / institution | Medical audit, clinical governance, service rules |
| Society and law | Courts, consumer protection, medicolegal duties, notification |
| Self | Conscience, reflection, continuing professional development |

**Relationship**

Integrity = internal moral compass; accountability = external answerability. Together they ensure **patient safety** and **public trust**.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 1; BMDC Code of Professional Conduct, Etiquette and Ethics.

## DU.7
**Definitions**

1. **Winslow (1920)**: the science and art of preventing disease, prolonging life and promoting physical health and efficiency through organised community efforts for:
   - sanitation of the environment
   - control of community infections
   - education of the individual in personal hygiene
   - organisation of medical and nursing services for early diagnosis and preventive treatment of disease
   - development of social machinery that ensures every individual a standard of living adequate for health,
   so that every citizen can realise his birthright of health and longevity.
2. **Acheson (UK, 1988; used by WHO)**: "the science and art of preventing disease, prolonging life and promoting health through the organized efforts of society."

**Key elements**

1. **Population** focus, not individual.
2. **Organised, collective** action by society and the state.
3. Prevention and promotion first.
4. **Equity** and social justice.
5. **Multidisciplinary and intersectoral**.

**Scope / functions**

1. Monitoring health status: surveillance, community diagnosis.
2. Control of communicable and non-communicable diseases.
3. Environmental health: water, sanitation, housing, pollution.
4. Health promotion and education.
5. MCH, family planning, nutrition.
6. Health policy, planning, legislation and regulation.
7. Organisation of health services (PHC) and workforce.
8. Emergency preparedness and response (International Health Regulations, 2005).
9. Research and evaluation.

**Public health vs community medicine**

Public health = broad, multisectoral practice of society; community medicine = medical discipline that applies it.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 1.

## DU.8
**Phases (Park)**

| Phase | Period | Main features |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Disease control phase | 1880–1920 | Sanitary legislation; control of environment: water supply, sewage and refuse disposal, drainage, housing; quarantine; germ theory gave scientific basis |
| 2. Health promotional phase | 1920–1960 | Shift from environment to the **individual**; personal health services: MCH, school health, industrial health, mental health, health education; health centres, basic health services |
| 3. Social engineering phase | 1960–1980 | Rise of chronic diseases, accidents, drug and alcohol problems; **social and behavioural** causes recognised; social medicine, social welfare, care of "at-risk" groups |
| 4. Health for All phase | 1981–2000 AD | Goal of **HFA by 2000**; **PHC** as key strategy |

**4th phase: Health for All (details)**

1. Recognition of a **health gap**: services concentrated in cities, hospitals and the well-off; rural poor uncovered.
2. **WHA30.43 (1977)**: main social target = a level of health permitting a socially and economically productive life by 2000.
3. **Alma-Ata Declaration (1978)**: **primary health care** as the key to HFA.
4. Principles: **equitable distribution, community participation, intersectoral coordination, appropriate technology**.
5. Shifts: curative → preventive/promotive; urban → rural; hospital → community; doctor-centred → team- and people-centred.
6. Health as a **fundamental human right** and part of socio-economic development.
7. Bangladesh: Upazila Health Complexes, union-level facilities, EPI (1979), ORS.

**After 2000 (updated: UN/WHO)**

MDGs (2000–2015) → SDGs (2016–2030; **SDG 3**, target 3.8 **UHC**) → **Astana Declaration (2018)** renewing PHC.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 1.

## DU.9
**Period**

1880–1920; first phase of the changing concept of public health.

**Background**

- Industrial Revolution (Europe) → rapid urbanisation, overcrowded slums, filth, polluted water.
- Repeated epidemics: cholera, typhoid, typhus, plague, smallpox; high death rates.
- **Sanitary awakening**: Chadwick's report on the labouring population (1842) → **Public Health Act 1848** (England); John Snow's cholera study (1854).
- **Germ theory** (Pasteur, Koch) gave a scientific basis to control.

**Main features**

1. Public health = **sanitary legislation** + **environmental control**.
2. Measures:
   - Safe water supply
   - Sewage, excreta and refuse disposal; drainage
   - Housing and slum regulation; removal of nuisances
   - Food hygiene and anti-adulteration laws
   - Quarantine, isolation, disinfection, notification
   - Vector control (malaria, yellow fever, plague)
   - Smallpox vaccination
3. Run by local sanitary authorities and Medical Officers of Health; international sanitary conferences for quarantine.

**Achievements**

1. Cholera, typhoid and plague largely controlled in Europe and North America **before** antibiotics and most vaccines.
2. Fall in death rates; rise in life expectancy.
3. Legal and administrative foundation of modern public health.

**Limitations**

1. Focus on environment, not on **man** himself.
2. No personal health services (MCH, school health).
3. Health education neglected.
4. Preventive and curative services separate.
5. Social and non-communicable problems ignored.

Disease control phase → **Health promotional phase** (1920–1960).

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 1.

## DU.10
**Definition**

- **Ottawa Charter (WHO, 1986)**: "Health promotion is the process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health."
- Park: first level of **primary prevention** (Leavell and Clark); not aimed at any one disease; strengthens the host by general measures.

**Place in public health**

**Health promotional phase (1920–1960)**: focus moved from environment to the **individual**; personal health services (MCH, school, industrial and mental health), health education, health centres.

**Approaches (Park)**

1. **Health education**: most cost-effective.
2. **Environmental modifications**: safe water, sanitation, housing, pollution control.
3. **Nutritional interventions**: fortification, supplementation, nutrition education.
4. **Lifestyle and behavioural change**: tobacco, alcohol, diet, exercise.

**Ottawa Charter: five action areas**

1. Build healthy public policy
2. Create supportive environments
3. Strengthen community action
4. Develop personal skills
5. Reorient health services

**Basic strategies**: Advocate, Enable, Mediate.
**Prerequisites for health**: peace, shelter, education, food, income, stable ecosystem, sustainable resources, social justice and equity.

**Later global conferences (WHO)**

Adelaide 1988 (healthy public policy) → Sundsvall 1991 (supportive environments) → Jakarta 1997 → Mexico 2000 → Bangkok 2005 → Nairobi 2009 → Helsinki 2013 (**Health in All Policies**) → Shanghai 2016 (good governance, healthy cities, health literacy) → Geneva 2021 (well-being societies).

**Bangladesh examples**

Tobacco control law (2005, amended 2013), salt iodisation, vitamin A campaigns, hand-washing promotion, Bureau of Health Education (DGHS).

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 1 & 2; WHO, Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion, 1986.

## DU.11
**Period**

1960–1980; third phase (Park: **social engineering phase**).

**Background**

1. Communicable diseases declined in developed countries (sanitation, vaccines, antibiotics).
2. New problems: cancer, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, mental illness, accidents, drug and alcohol abuse, juvenile delinquency.
3. These have **social and behavioural** causes; vaccines and sanitation alone cannot control them.
4. Rising cost of medical care; inequity between rich and poor.

**Features**

- Health seen as a **social** as well as biological phenomenon (social medicine).
- Attention to social, economic, behavioural and cultural determinants.
- Expansion of social welfare: social security, health insurance, welfare services.
- Identification of **"at-risk" groups** and preventive care for them.
- Behavioural sciences (sociology, psychology) entered medical teaching.

**Importance**

1. Broadened health beyond the biomedical → **psychosocial and holistic** concepts.
2. Recognised poverty, illiteracy, unemployment and bad housing as **causes of ill health** → social determinants approach.
3. Base for **chronic disease epidemiology** and lifestyle modification.
4. Introduced **social security and health insurance** for workers, elderly and disabled.
5. Emphasised **equity** and care of the vulnerable.
6. Promoted **community participation** and **intersectoral action** (education, agriculture, housing).
7. Linked health with socio-economic **development**.
8. Led directly to the **Health for All** phase and **PHC** (Alma-Ata, 1978).

**Bangladesh relevance**

NCDs now rising; social measures (girls' education, poverty reduction, social safety nets) improve health more than medical care alone.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 1.

## DU.12
**Latest phase (Park)**

**Health for All phase (1981–2000 AD)**: fourth phase of the changing concept of public health.

**Background**

- Services reached mainly urban, rich and hospital users; rural poor left out (**health gap**).
- Health accepted as a human right and part of development.

**Goal: Health for All by 2000 AD**

- **WHA30.43 (1977)**: main social target of governments and WHO = attainment by all citizens of the world by 2000 of a level of health that permits a socially and economically productive life.
- **Alma-Ata Declaration (1978)**: **primary health care** = key to HFA.

**Features**

1. Health as a **fundamental human right**.
2. Shift: curative → preventive/promotive; hospital → community; urban → rural.
3. PHC principles: **equitable distribution, community participation, intersectoral coordination, appropriate technology**.
4. Eight essential elements of PHC (education, food and nutrition, water and sanitation, MCH and FP, immunisation, local endemic disease control, treatment of common diseases, essential drugs).
5. Global indicators to monitor progress (e.g., IMR <50/1000 LB, life expectancy >60 years, adult literacy >70%).

**Health-related goals after 2000 (updated: UN/WHO)**

| Framework | Period | Health-related goal |
|---|---|---|
| HFA policy for 21st century (WHA51.7) | 1998 | Renewed HFA |
| MDGs | 2000–2015 | Goals 4, 5, 6 (+ targets 1C, 7C, 8E) |
| SDGs | 2016–2030 | **SDG 3**: healthy lives and well-being for all at all ages; target 3.8 **UHC** |
| Astana Declaration | 2018 | Renewed PHC as the route to UHC and the health SDGs |

Current form of HFA = **UHC through PHC** under **SDG 3** by 2030.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 1 & 7.

## DU.13
**Definitions**

1. **WHO (Constitution, 1948)**: "Health is a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity."
2. **Amplified (Alma-Ata, 1978)**: health includes the ability to lead a **socially and economically productive life**.
3. **Operational definition (WHO study group)**:
   - Broad sense: a condition or quality of the organism showing **adequate functioning** under given genetic and environmental conditions.
   - Narrow sense (for measurement): no obvious disease; body functions within normal limits for age, sex, community and region; organs in balance (**homeostasis**).
4. **Ottawa Charter (1986)**: health is a **resource for everyday life**, not the objective of living.
5. **Huber et al. (BMJ, 2011)**: health as the ability to **adapt and self-manage** in face of social, physical and emotional challenges.

**WHO definition: merits and criticisms**

| Merits | Criticisms |
|---|---|
| Broad, positive concept | "Complete" well-being is unattainable, idealistic |
| Includes mental and social aspects | Not measurable; no operational value |
| Sets a goal for health services | Ignores spiritual dimension |
| Basis of holistic approach | Health is a dynamic process, not a static "state" |

**New philosophy of health (Park)**

1. Health is a **fundamental human right**.
2. Health is the essence of productive life, not the result of ever-rising spending on medical care.
3. Health is **intersectoral**.
4. Health is an integral part of **development**.
5. Health is central to **quality of life**.
6. Health involves individual, state and international responsibility.
7. Health and its maintenance is a major **social investment**.
8. Health is a **world-wide social goal**.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 2; WHO Constitution, 1948.

## DU.14
**Changing concepts of health (Park)**

Biomedical → Ecological → Psychosocial → Holistic

| Concept | Health seen as | Key points | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. **Biomedical** | Absence of disease | Based on germ theory; body as a machine, disease as breakdown, doctor as repairer; dominant till mid-20th century | Ignores environmental, social, psychological and cultural factors; failed against malnutrition, chronic disease, mental illness, pollution, population growth |
| 2. **Ecological** | Dynamic equilibrium between man and environment | Disease = maladjustment of man to environment; imperfect man in an imperfect environment (Dubos); health = relative absence of pain and discomfort with continuous adaptation | Incomplete without social and psychological aspects |
| 3. **Psychosocial** | Biological and social phenomenon | Health shaped by social, psychological, cultural, economic and political factors | Needs to be joined with biological and environmental views |
| 4. **Holistic** | Multidimensional well-being of the whole person in his environment | Synthesis of all earlier concepts; social, economic, political and environmental influences; many sectors (agriculture, education, industry, housing, communication) affect health; stress on promotion and protection | Demands intersectoral effort; hard to measure |

**Significance**

1. Moved focus from **disease to health**, from **cure to prevention and promotion**.
2. From **individual to community**; from doctor alone to **team and other sectors**.
3. The WHO definition (1948) and PHC (Alma-Ata, 1978) reflect the **holistic** concept.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 2.

## DU.15
**Meaning**

**Holistic concept** = synthesis of biomedical, ecological and psychosocial concepts. Health is a **multidimensional** process involving the well-being of the **whole person** (body, mind, social life, spirit) in the context of his **environment**.

**What it implies**

1. Treat the **person, not only the disease**: physical, mental, social and spiritual needs.
2. Many **determinants**: biology, behaviour, environment, socio-economic status, health services.
3. **Intersectoral approach**: agriculture, food, education, water, housing, industry, communication and social welfare all affect health.
4. **All levels of prevention**: promotion, specific protection, early treatment, disability limitation, rehabilitation.
5. **Comprehensive, continuous, family-centred** care.
6. **Community participation** and self-care.
7. **Team approach**: doctor, nurse, health worker, social worker, nutritionist.
8. **Integrated programmes**: PHC, IMCI, essential service package; **One Health** (human–animal–environment).
9. Priority to **promotion and protection** of health.

**Example**

Type 2 diabetes managed holistically: drugs + diet + exercise + foot and eye care + counselling for stress or depression + family support + workplace adjustment + community screening and awareness.

**Biomedical vs holistic approach**

| Biomedical | Holistic |
|---|---|
| Disease-centred | Person-centred |
| Single cause | Multiple causes |
| Cure | Promotion, prevention, cure, rehabilitation |
| Doctor alone | Team, community and other sectors |
| Hospital-based | Home, community and facility |

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 2.

## DU.16
**Five major dimensions**

| Dimension | Meaning / signs |
|---|---|
| 1. **Physical** | Perfect functioning of the body: good complexion, clean skin, bright eyes, good appetite, sound sleep, normal pulse and BP for age and sex |
| 2. **Mental** | Balance between self and surroundings: free of inner conflict, well adjusted, good self-esteem and self-control, solves problems |
| 3. **Social** | Quantity and quality of ties with family and community; social skills and roles |
| 4. **Spiritual** | Meaning and purpose in life; principles, ethics, faith |
| 5. **Emotional** | "Feeling": freedom from anxiety and depression; handling emotions (mental = "knowing") |

**Other dimensions**

Vocational (work satisfaction); philosophical, cultural, socio-economic, environmental, educational, nutritional, curative, preventive.

**Health is multidimensional: justification**

1. The WHO definition itself names **physical, mental and social** well-being.
2. Dimensions **interact**: chronic illness → depression → job loss → poverty → poor nutrition → more illness.
3. Across cultures health has always meant more than a sound body:
   - **Ayurveda**: balance of body humours, senses, mind and soul.
   - **Unani** (Greek–Arab): balance of four humours and temperament.
   - **Islamic tradition**: well-being of body, mind and soul.
   - **Chinese medicine**: harmony of yin and yang with nature.
   - Folk and tribal systems: harmony with family, ancestors and community.
4. Lay people judge health by ability to **work, perform social roles and feel well**, not only by absence of disease.
5. So health is **measured** by many indicators (mortality, morbidity, disability, quality of life) and **promoted** by holistic, intersectoral action.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 2.

## DU.17
**Definition**

- Mental health is more than the absence of mental illness.
- **WHO (2022)**: "a state of mental well-being that enables people to cope with the stresses of life, realize their abilities, learn well and work well, and contribute to their community."
- Park: a state of **balance** between the individual and the world around him; harmony with self, others and environment.

**Characteristics of a mentally healthy person (Park)**

1. Free from internal **conflicts**.
2. **Well adjusted**; gets along with others; accepts criticism.
3. Searches for **identity**.
4. Strong sense of **self-esteem**.
5. Knows himself, his needs, problems and goals (**self-actualisation**).
6. Good **self-control**; balances reason and emotion.
7. Faces problems and solves them **intelligently**; copes with stress.

**Determinants**

- Biological: genetics, brain disease, substance use.
- Psychological: personality, coping skills, childhood experiences.
- Social: poverty, unemployment, violence, discrimination, family conflict, migration, disasters.

**Assessment**

Clinical interview; mental status examination; psychological tests (intelligence, personality); screening scales (GHQ-12, PHQ-9, GAD-7, WHO-5).

**Importance**

1. "No health without mental health": mind and body interact (psychosomatic disorders: peptic ulcer, hypertension, asthma).
2. Mental disorders cause major disability and loss of productivity.
3. Depression worsens outcome of diabetes, CVD, TB, HIV.
4. Promotion: life skills, school mental health, stress management, social support.
5. Bangladesh: **Mental Health Act 2018**; mental health integrated into PHC.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 2 & 15; WHO, Mental health: strengthening our response, 2022.

## DU.19
**Concept**

Physical health = "perfect functioning" of the body; every cell and organ works at optimum capacity in harmony with the rest.

**Signs of physical health**

Good complexion; clean skin; bright eyes; lustrous hair; firm flesh, not too fat; sweet breath; good appetite; sound sleep; regular bowel and bladder; smooth, easy, coordinated movements; organs of normal size and function; intact special senses; resting pulse, BP and exercise tolerance in normal range for age and sex.

**Evaluation at individual level**

1. Self-assessment of overall health.
2. Inquiry into symptoms of ill health and risk factors (tobacco, alcohol, diet).
3. Inquiry into medications.
4. Inquiry into level of physical activity.
5. Inquiry into use of medical services.
6. Standardised questionnaires (e.g., for cardiovascular disease).
7. Standardised physical examination.
8. Physiological tests: pulse, BP, ECG, lung function, exercise tolerance.
9. Nutritional assessment: anthropometry (height, weight, BMI, MUAC, waist), diet survey, clinical signs.
10. Biochemical and laboratory tests: haemoglobin, blood glucose, lipids, urine, stool.

**Evaluation at community level**

Health status indicators: CDR, IMR, U5MR, life expectancy, morbidity (incidence, prevalence), disability rates, nutritional status.

## DU.20
**Concept**

Positive health = perfect functioning of body and mind, beyond mere freedom from disease:
- **Biologically**: every cell and organ works at optimum capacity, in perfect harmony with the rest.
- **Psychologically**: the person feels perfect well-being and mastery over his environment.
- **Socially**: optimum capacity to take part in the social system.

**Characteristics**

1. Positive, not negative ("not only no disease").
2. Dynamic, not static; changes with age, environment and culture.
3. An ideal that can never be fully reached (a "mirage"), since all its factors keep changing.
4. So the practical goal is **"better health"** for all, not perfect health.
5. Needs personal effort (lifestyle) plus community and social action.

**Spectrum of health (health continuum)**

Positive health → Better health → Freedom from sickness → Unrecognised sickness → Mild sickness → Severe sickness → Death

- Health and disease lie on a continuum; a person's position keeps shifting.
- Much sickness is unrecognised (iceberg of disease).

**Moving towards positive health**

1. Health promotion: balanced diet, exercise, sleep, no tobacco or alcohol, stress control.
2. Specific protection: immunisation, safe water, sanitation.
3. Periodic health check-up and screening.
4. Mental and social well-being: family, social support, meaningful work.
5. Healthy public policy and supportive environment.

**Measures reflecting positive health**

Life expectancy, health-adjusted life expectancy (HALE), quality-of-life scores, physical fitness tests.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 2.

## DU.21
**Meaning**

Well-being = overall welfare of a person or community; complex, partly subjective. Two components (Park):

| Component | Expressed as | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| **Objective** | **Standard of living / level of living** | Measurable material conditions |
| **Subjective** | **Quality of life** | What each person perceives and feels |

**1. Standard of living**

Usual scale of daily living: income, occupation, housing, sanitation, nutrition, and provision of health, educational, recreational and other services. Depends on per capita income.

**2. Level of living (UN): 9 components**

Health; food consumption; education; occupation and working conditions; housing; social security; clothing; recreation and leisure; human rights. Health is the most important.

**3. Quality of life**

**WHO (WHOQOL Group, 1995)**: "individuals' perception of their position in life in the context of the culture and value systems in which they live and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards and concerns."

**Composite indices**

| Index | Components | Range |
|---|---|---|
| **PQLI** | Infant mortality, life expectancy at age 1, literacy | 0–100 |
| **HDI** (UNDP) | Long and healthy life (life expectancy at birth); knowledge (mean and expected years of schooling); decent standard of living (GNI per capita, PPP $) | 0–1 |

Bangladesh HDI **0.685**, rank **130 of 193**, medium human development (UNDP HDR 2025, 2023 data).

## DU.22
**Concept**

Well-being = objective component (standard / level of living) + **subjective component** (what the person perceives) = **quality of life**.

**Quality of life (WHO, 1995)**: "individuals' perception of their position in life in the context of the culture and value systems in which they live and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards and concerns."

**Subjective components**

| Component | Illustration |
|---|---|
| Happiness / positive feelings | Joy, contentment, enthusiasm |
| Absence of negative feelings | Little anxiety, sadness, anger |
| Life satisfaction | Judging that life is going well overall |
| Meaning and purpose | Goals, values, faith (spiritual) |
| Self-esteem and self-acceptance | Positive view of oneself |
| Sense of control (autonomy) | Feeling able to shape one's own life |
| Social connectedness | Satisfying relations with family, friends, community |
| Perceived health | Self-rated physical and mental health |
| Satisfaction with environment | Safety, housing, services, freedom |

**WHOQOL domains**

- WHOQOL-100 (six): physical health; psychological; level of independence; social relationships; environment; spirituality / religion / personal beliefs.

## DU.23
**Concept**

- Health = **absence of disease**; a person without disease is healthy.
- Rooted in the **germ theory** (Pasteur, Koch, late 19th century); dominated medicine until mid-20th century.
- Body seen as a **machine**; disease = breakdown of the machine; doctor = repairer.
- Disease explained by biological agents and abnormalities (pathology, physiology, biochemistry).

**Features**

1. Single-cause (agent) model of disease.
2. Hospital- and doctor-centred; curative.
3. Reliance on medical technology, drugs and surgery.
4. Health judged by clinical and laboratory findings.
5. Mind and body treated separately.

**Contributions**

1. Discovery of microbes, vaccines and antibiotics; control of many infections.
2. Scientific diagnosis and treatment; growth of specialties.

**Limitations**

1. Ignores **environmental, social, psychological and cultural** determinants.
2. Health is more than absence of disease; a person may have no disease yet feel unwell or be socially unfit.
3. Cannot solve malnutrition, chronic disease, mental illness, accidents, drug abuse, pollution, population explosion.
4. Costly; widens inequity; neglects prevention and promotion.
5. Neglects the patient's experience and quality of life.

**Outcome**

Its limitations led to newer concepts:
Biomedical → Ecological → Psychosocial → Holistic

**Example**

Treating a malnourished child's diarrhoea with ORS and drugs alone, without addressing water, sanitation, feeding and poverty → repeated illness.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 2.

## DU.24
**Definition**

Determinants of health = factors inside and outside the body whose interaction decides the health of individuals and communities.

**Determinants (Park)**

| Determinant | Examples |
|---|---|
| 1. **Biological** | Genetic make-up; chromosomal anomalies (Down syndrome); inborn errors (thalassaemia); predisposition (diabetes, hypertension); age, sex |
| 2. **Behavioural and socio-cultural** | Lifestyle: smoking, diet, physical activity, alcohol, sexual behaviour; customs, beliefs |
| 3. **Environment** | Internal milieu; external: physical (water, air, housing, arsenic), biological (vectors, microbes), psychosocial (stress, support) |
| 4. **Socio-economic** | Economic status, education (esp. female), occupation, political system |
| 5. **Health services** | Coverage, access and quality: immunisation, ANC, safe delivery, essential drugs |
| 6. **Ageing of population** | More NCDs, disability, care needs |
| 7. **Gender** | Unequal access to food, education, care; gender-based violence |
| 8. **Other factors** | Food and agriculture, education, industry, social welfare, rural development; information and communication technology |

**Biological vs social determinants**

- **Biological**: largely non-modifiable (genes, age, sex); some managed by genetic counselling and screening.
- **Social determinants (WHO)**: non-medical conditions in which people are born, grow, work, live and age, and the wider forces (economics, policies, social norms, politics) shaping them; **modifiable**; root of health inequities.

**WHO framework (CSDH, 2008)**

Structural determinants (socio-political context; income, education, occupation, gender, ethnicity) → Intermediary determinants (living and working conditions, behaviours, psychosocial factors, health system) → Health and equity

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 2; WHO, Closing the gap in a generation (CSDH), 2008.

## DU.25
**Health indicator**

A variable that helps measure changes in health status, directly or indirectly.

**Ideal characteristics (Park)**

| Characteristic | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Valid** | Measures what it is meant to measure | IMR reflects child health and MCH care |
| 2. **Reliable and objective** | Same result when measured by different people at different times under similar conditions | Birth weight on a calibrated scale |
| 3. **Sensitive** | Responds to changes in the situation | IMR falls quickly as MCH services improve |
| 4. **Specific** | Reflects changes only in the situation concerned | Neonatal tetanus rate reflects TT coverage and clean delivery |
| 5. **Feasible** | Data obtainable easily at acceptable cost | Measles vaccine coverage from EPI records |
| 6. **Relevant** | Adds to understanding of the problem of interest | MMR for a maternal health programme |

**Additional desirable qualities (WHO / SMART)**

- **S**pecific, **M**easurable, **A**chievable, **R**elevant, **T**ime-bound.
- Comparable across places and over time.
- Simple and easily understood.
- Timely and regularly available.
- Can be disaggregated (age, sex, residence, wealth) to show inequity.
- Acceptable and ethical to collect.

**Note**

No single indicator meets every criterion; a **set (profile)** of indicators is used.
Five characteristics (short answer): valid, reliable, sensitive, specific, feasible (also relevant).

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 2.

## DU.26
**A. Socio-economic conditions**

1. **Economic status**
   - Per capita income decides purchasing power, standard of living, family size and disease pattern.
   - Poverty → malnutrition, overcrowding, unsafe water, infections (TB, diarrhoea), low use of services, high IMR.
   - Affluence → obesity, diabetes, coronary heart disease.
2. **Education**
   - **Female literacy** → lower IMR and fertility; better hygiene, feeding, immunisation, care-seeking.
   - Educated people understand and act on health messages.
3. **Occupation**
   - Work gives income, status, self-esteem; unemployment → poverty, mental illness, substance use, suicide.
   - Occupational hazards: silicosis, pesticide poisoning, injuries (garment, brick-kiln, ship-breaking workers).
4. **Political system**
   - Decides health budget, policy, resource distribution, land reform, social safety nets, commitment to PHC and UHC.

**B. Cultural conditions (customs, beliefs, traditions, values)**

| Harmful to health | Helpful to health |
|---|---|
| Child marriage → teenage pregnancy, maternal and neonatal deaths | Tradition of breastfeeding |
| Food taboos in pregnancy; prelacteal feeds; discarding colostrum | Religious cleanliness (ablution, hand-washing) |
| Consanguineous marriage → thalassaemia, other genetic disorders | Joint family care of elderly and sick |
| Home delivery by untrained attendants; restricted mobility of women | Abstinence from alcohol in many communities |
| Belief in supernatural causes; quacks; delayed treatment | Community solidarity in crises |
| Son preference; discrimination against girls in food and care | |
| Betel-quid and smokeless tobacco → oral cancer | |

**C. Interaction**

Poverty → illiteracy → harmful practices → ill health → loss of work → deeper poverty (vicious cycle)

**Implications**

Health education, female education, poverty alleviation, social safety nets, culturally acceptable services, community participation.

## DU.27
**Definition**

- **Community participation**: process by which individuals and families take responsibility for their own health and that of their community, and build the capacity to contribute to its development (Alma-Ata, 1978).
- Park: involvement of the community in **planning, implementation and maintenance** of health services, with maximum use of **local resources** (manpower, money, materials).

**Place in PHC**

Principles of PHC: equitable distribution; **community participation**; intersectoral coordination; appropriate technology.

**Role in achieving HFA**

1. Health cannot simply be "delivered"; people must take responsibility (**self-reliance**).
2. Services become relevant, acceptable and culturally appropriate.
3. Local resources lower cost and make services **sustainable**.
4. Better coverage and use: immunisation, ANC, family planning, sanitation.
5. Makes providers **accountable** to the people.
6. **Empowers** people; promotes equity.

**How the community can participate**

1. Identify needs and priorities (community diagnosis).
2. Plan and manage services through health committees.
3. Contribute land, labour, money and materials.
4. Serve as volunteers and community health workers.
5. Practise and spread healthy behaviour in families.
6. Support campaigns: NIDs, vitamin A, sanitation, mosquito control.
7. Monitor quality and give feedback.

**Bangladesh example: Community Clinic**

- Built on **land donated by the community**; government provides building, staff (CHCP), drugs and logistics.
- Managed by a **Community Group (CG)**, helped by three **Community Support Groups (CSGs)** for awareness, referral and local funds.
- Planned as one clinic per ~6,000 people within 30 minutes' walk (CCHST, 2025).
- Others: NGO volunteers (e.g., BRAC *shasthya shebika*), union parishad health committees.

## DU.29
**Why mortality indicators are good indicators**

1. Death is a definite event → **objective and reliable**.
2. Data easily available (vital registration, SVRS, surveys, census) → **feasible**.
3. Recorded everywhere → **comparable** across countries and over time.
4. Reflect the end result of all determinants (socio-economic, environmental, nutritional, health services) → **valid** summary of health.
5. Oldest and still most widely used indicators.

**Justification of each indicator**

| Indicator | Why it is a fair and good indicator |
|---|---|
| Crude death rate | Simple overall measure; but affected by age structure, so poor for comparison unless standardised |
| **Infant mortality rate** | Most sensitive index of health status and level of living; reflects MCH care, nutrition, sanitation, female education; responds quickly to change |
| Under-5 mortality rate | Reflects child survival, nutrition, immunisation; MDG/SDG indicator |
| Child mortality rate (1–4 y) | Reflects environment, nutrition, infections |
| **Maternal mortality ratio** | Reflects quality of obstetric care, access to services, status of women |
| **Life expectancy** | Best single summary of mortality at all ages; free of age-structure effect; good index of socio-economic development |
| Disease-specific mortality | Burden of a disease; evaluates control programmes |
| Proportional mortality rate | Simple; e.g., share of deaths from communicable diseases, share of deaths at age ≥50 years |

**Limitations**

1. Show only death, not illness, disability or quality of life (tip of the iceberg).
2. As mortality falls they become **less sensitive**; NCDs and mental illness poorly reflected.
3. Under-registration and wrong cause-of-death coding in developing countries.
4. National averages hide inequalities unless disaggregated.

So they are combined with morbidity, disability (DALY, HALE) and quality-of-life indicators.
Example: Bangladesh IMR 27 per 1000 live births (SVRS 2023).

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 2; BBS SVRS 2023 (The Financial Express, 2024), https://today.thefinancialexpress.com.bd/print/life-expectancy-falls-in-bd-bbs-1724781053

## DU.30
**Definition**

**Health for All (HFA)**: attainment by all citizens of the world by the year 2000 of a level of health that will permit them to lead a **socially and economically productive life** (World Health Assembly, WHA30.43, 1977).

**Background**

- WHA 1977: HFA declared the main social target of governments and WHO.
- **Alma-Ata Declaration (1978)**: **primary health care** = key to HFA.
- WHA 1981: Global Strategy for HFA by 2000, with global indicators.

**What HFA means**

1. Health begins at home, school and workplace.
2. People use better ways to prevent illness and relieve unavoidable disease and disability; grow up, grow old and die gracefully.
3. Health resources are evenly distributed.
4. Essential health care is accessible to all individuals and families, in an acceptable and affordable way, with their full involvement.
5. People realise disease is not inevitable and that they can shape their own lives.

**What HFA does NOT mean**

- That doctors and nurses will treat everyone for every illness by 2000.
- That nobody will be sick or disabled by 2000.

**Strategy**

PHC: 8 essential elements; 4 principles (equitable distribution, community participation, intersectoral coordination, appropriate technology).

**Monitoring**

12 global indicators, e.g., IMR <50/1000 LB, life expectancy >60 years, adult literacy >70%, at least 5% of GNP on health.

**After 2000 (updated: WHO/UN)**

HFA policy for the 21st century (WHA51.7, 1998) → MDGs (2000–2015) → **SDG 3 and UHC** (2016–2030) → **Astana Declaration (2018)**: PHC as the path to UHC.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 1 & 7.

## DU.31
**Global indicators for HFA by 2000 (WHO, Global Strategy, 1981)**

1. HFA endorsed as policy at the **highest official level**.
2. Mechanisms for **involving people** in implementing strategies formed or strengthened, and functioning.
3. At least **5% of GNP** spent on health.
4. A reasonable share of national health expenditure on **local health care** (first-level care).

10. **Life expectancy at birth >60 years**.
11. **Adult literacy >70%** for both men and women.
12. **GNP per head >US$ 500**.

## DU.33
**Concept**

- **MDGs**: eight time-bound, measurable development goals from the **UN Millennium Declaration**, signed by 189 member states at the Millennium Summit (September 2000).
- Aim: halve extreme poverty and improve human development by **2015**, from a **1990 baseline**.
- Framework: **8 goals, 21 targets, 60 indicators** (revised 2007; originally 18 targets, 48 indicators).
- Shared responsibility: developing countries (policy, governance) + developed countries (aid, trade, debt relief; Goal 8).

**The eight goals**

1. Eradicate extreme poverty and hunger
2. Achieve universal primary education
3. Promote gender equality and empower women
4. Reduce child mortality
5. Improve maternal health
6. Combat HIV/AIDS, malaria and other diseases
7. Ensure environmental sustainability
8. Develop a global partnership for development

**Health in MDGs**

3 goals directly on health (4, 5, 6); health targets also in Goals 1 (hunger), 7 (water, sanitation) and 8 (essential drugs).

**Features**

1. Simple, clear, quantified, time-bound; easy to monitor.
2. Mobilised political will and funds (GAVI 2000, Global Fund 2002).
3. Focus on developing countries.

**Limitations**

1. Narrow: NCDs, mental health, injuries, health systems left out.
2. Donor-driven; national averages hid inequalities.

**Outcome**

- World missed the U5MR target; 24 low- and lower-middle-income countries met it, including **Bangladesh** (UNICEF, 2015).
- Bangladesh U5MR 46 vs target 48 (BDHS 2014).

**Successor**

**SDGs** (September 2015): 17 goals, 169 targets, 2016–2030; universal; health in **SDG 3**.

## DU.34
**Health indicator**

A variable that measures changes in health status, directly or indirectly, and shows how far objectives and targets are being reached.

**Four important aspects (uses)**

1. **Measure the health status** of a community
   - Community diagnosis; e.g., IMR, life expectancy, stunting.
2. **Compare** health status
   - Between countries, regions, groups (urban–rural, rich–poor) and over time.
3. **Assess health care needs and allocate resources**
   - Identify priority problems and underserved groups; guide planning and allocation of scarce resources.
4. **Monitor and evaluate** health services and programmes
   - e.g., immunisation coverage for EPI; ANC and skilled birth attendance for maternal health; progress towards SDG 3.

**Qualities that make these uses possible**

Valid; reliable and objective; sensitive; specific; feasible; relevant.

**Types (brief)**

Mortality; morbidity; disability; nutritional status; health care delivery; utilisation; social and mental health; environmental; socio-economic; health policy; quality of life; other (HFA, basic needs, SDG indicators).

**Other aspects**

- Indicators are usually expressed as **rates, ratios or proportions**.
- A **set** of indicators is needed; no single indicator captures health.
- Support **advocacy and accountability** of governments and programmes.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 2.

## DU.36

## DU.37
**Concept**

- **Comprehensive health care**: integrated provision of **promotive, preventive, curative and rehabilitative** (and palliative) services to the individual, family and community through one system, at all levels.
- Origin: **Bhore Committee (India, 1946)**: integration of curative and preventive medicine at all levels; later the basis of PHC.
- Treats the whole person (holistic), with continuity from birth to death.

**Features**

1. **Integration** of preventive and curative services (not separate vertical units).
2. **Continuity** of care, "womb to tomb", across levels through **referral**.
3. **Whole population** covered, priority to vulnerable groups.
4. **Team approach**: doctors, nurses, health assistants, CHCPs, social workers.
5. **Community participation** and **intersectoral** links (water, education, agriculture).
6. Covers physical, mental and social health.
7. First contact close to people, at primary level.
8. Rational, cost-effective use of resources.

**Components**

| Component | Example |
|---|---|
| Promotive | Health education, nutrition, sanitation |
| Preventive | EPI, ANC, vitamin A, screening |
| Curative | Treatment of pneumonia, TB, hypertension |
| Rehabilitative | Physiotherapy, artificial limbs, vocational training |
| Palliative | Pain relief in cancer |

**Bangladesh**

- Community clinic: one-stop health, family planning and nutrition services.
- Upazila Health Complex: integrated preventive, curative and family planning services for the upazila.
- **Essential Service Package (ESP)** under the health sector programmes.

**Contrast**

Fragmented, episodic care treats only the disease; comprehensive care treats the person and the community.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 2 & 23.

## DU.38
**Components of comprehensive health care**

| Component | Aim | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Health promotion** | Improve general health and well-being | Health education, nutrition, healthy lifestyle, sanitation, housing |
| 2. **Specific protection** | Protect against particular diseases | Immunisation (EPI), chemoprophylaxis, iodised salt, vitamin A, helmets and seat belts, protective gear at work |
| 3. **Early diagnosis and treatment** | Detect and treat early; stop spread and complications | Screening (BP, diabetes, VIA for cervical cancer), TB case finding, malaria treatment, IMCI |
| 4. **Disability limitation** | Prevent progression to disability or death | Good diabetes control to avoid amputation; stroke care; care of leprosy reactions |
| 5. **Rehabilitation** | Restore highest possible function | Physiotherapy, artificial limbs, hearing aids, vocational training, social support |
| 6. **Palliative care** | Relieve suffering in incurable illness | Oral morphine for cancer pain, home-based care |

Components 1–5 = Leavell and Clark's five modes of intervention; WHO adds palliative care to the UHC continuum.

**Grouping**

Promotive + preventive (1, 2) → Curative (3, 4) → Rehabilitative and palliative (5, 6)

**Example: tuberculosis**

1. Promotion: health education, nutrition, ventilation.
2. Protection: BCG; TB preventive therapy for contacts.
3. Early diagnosis and treatment: sputum microscopy / GeneXpert; DOTS.
4. Disability limitation: management of complications and drug-resistant TB.
5. Rehabilitation: vocational and social support.

**Medical care vs health care**

| Point | Medical care | Health care |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Services by doctors and allied staff to diagnose and treat illness | All services by health professions and agencies to promote, maintain, monitor or restore health |
| Scope | Narrow; a part of health care | Broad; medical care + public health + related services |

## DU.40
**a) Type of referral (1)**

**Vertical (upward) referral**: stepwise from primary (community clinic) → first-referral level (Upazila Health Complex) → tertiary (medical college hospital); a **three-tier** referral.

**b) Types of referral in Bangladesh (3)**

Service tiers:
Community clinic (ward; ~6,000 people) → Union Health and Family Welfare Centre / Union Sub-centre → Upazila Health Complex → District hospital → Medical college hospital / specialised institute (e.g., National Institute of Diseases of the Chest and Hospital)

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Vertical: upward** | Lower to higher level for more skilled care | Severe asthma: CC → UHC → medical college hospital |
| 2. **Vertical: downward (back / reverse referral)** | Higher to lower level for follow-up after stabilisation | Stable asthmatic sent back to UHC or CC for inhaler follow-up |
| 3. **Horizontal (lateral)** | Between facilities or departments at the same level | Medicine to surgery within a district hospital; one UHC to another with a specialist |
| 4. **Diagonal** | To a different level and a different specialty | UHC medicine OPD → district hospital eye department |
| 5. **Self-referral (bypass)** | Patient goes straight to a higher level without referral | Common in Bangladesh; overcrowds tertiary hospitals |

Also by urgency: **emergency** vs **routine (elective)** referral. A proper referral needs a **referral slip** and **counter-referral (feedback)**.

**c) Importance of referral system (3)**

1. **Right care at the right level**; continuity of care.
2. **Gatekeeping**: keeps minor cases away from tertiary hospitals.
3. **Efficient use of resources**: specialists, beds and equipment for those who need them.
4. Lowers **cost** and travel for patients; most problems solved locally.
5. **Saves lives**: timely transfer of emergencies (obstetric, severe asthma, trauma).
6. **Strengthens PHC** and community trust in lower facilities.
7. **Two-way communication**: feedback improves skills of primary-level staff.
8. Better records, monitoring and quality of care.
9. Supports an integrated health system and **UHC**.

**Problems in Bangladesh**

Weak enforcement, frequent self-referral, poor transport and ambulance services, little feedback, staff and drug shortages at lower levels.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed., Ch. 23; Daily Observer (bdnews24), 2025, https://www.observerbd.com/news/540274

## DU.41
**Definition (Alma-Ata Declaration, 1978)**

"Primary health care is essential health care based on practical, scientifically sound and socially acceptable methods and technology made universally accessible to individuals and families in the community through their full participation and at a cost that the community and country can afford to maintain at every stage of their development in the spirit of self-reliance and self-determination."

It is part of both the national health system (its central function and main focus) and overall socio-economic development; the **first level of contact** of people with the health system, bringing care as close as possible to where they live and work; the first element of a **continuing health care process**.

**Updated concept (WHO and UNICEF, Astana 2018)**

A whole-of-society approach that aims at the highest, most equitable level of health and well-being, focused on people's needs, from promotion and prevention to treatment, rehabilitation and palliative care, as close as possible to everyday life. Three components:
1. Integrated health services (primary care + essential public health functions)
2. Multisectoral policy and action
3. Empowered people and communities

**Key features**

1. **Essential** care for common problems.
2. **Accessible, acceptable, affordable**.
3. **Community participation** and self-reliance.
4. **Appropriate technology**.
5. First contact; linked to referral.
6. Part of socio-economic development (**intersectoral**).

**Elements (8)**

Health education; food and nutrition; safe water and sanitation; MCH including family planning; immunisation; control of local endemic diseases; treatment of common diseases and injuries; essential drugs.

**Principles (4)**

Equitable distribution; community participation; intersectoral coordination; appropriate technology.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Health Care of the Community); WHO/UNICEF, Declaration of Astana, 2018.

## DU.43
**Best element for immediate improvement: "Education concerning prevailing health problems and the methods of preventing and controlling them" (health education)**

**Why**

1. **Key to all other elements**: people use immunisation, ANC, family planning, sanitation and ORS only when they understand them.
2. **Quick results**: messages spread fast through mass media, mobile phones, community clinics, schools, mosques, NGO workers.
3. **Cheapest and most cost-effective** intervention; needs few resources.
4. **Fits Bangladesh's problems**, which are largely behaviour-related: hand-washing, safe water, breastfeeding, child marriage, early ANC, tobacco, salt intake, dengue source reduction.
5. **Creates demand** for services and reduces delay in seeking care (e.g., danger signs in pregnancy and childhood pneumonia).
6. **Empowers communities**: promotes self-care, community participation and accountability.
7. **Proven success**: home-made ORS (lobon-gur), family planning and EPI campaigns spread through health education.
8. Addresses the **rising NCD burden**: diet, exercise, tobacco, BP and diabetes screening.

**Other strong choices**

- **Immunisation (EPI)**: rapid fall in child deaths; but coverage gaps remain.
- **MCH and family planning**: targets the most vulnerable.

**Conclusion line**

Health education multiplies the effect of every other element at the lowest cost.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Health Care of the Community; Communication for Health Education).

## DU.44
**PHC = essential health care: justification**

1. **By definition (Alma-Ata, 1978)**: PHC is "essential health care" based on practical, scientifically sound and socially acceptable methods.
2. **Meets basic health needs**: its 8 elements tackle the main causes of illness and death (infections, malnutrition, maternal and child problems, unsafe water).
3. **First contact**: most health problems (common illnesses, ANC, immunisation, FP) can be managed at this level.
4. **Universal accessibility**: brings care close to home, work and school.
5. **Affordable**: low cost for people and the country; uses local resources.
6. **Acceptable**: socially and culturally appropriate methods.
7. **Equity**: priority to the poor, rural and vulnerable.
8. **Comprehensive**: promotive, preventive, curative and rehabilitative.
9. **Community participation and self-reliance**: makes care sustainable.
10. **Cost-effective**: prevention is cheaper than hospital care.
11. **Foundation of the health system**: links people to secondary and tertiary care through referral.
12. **Route to UHC and SDG 3** (Astana Declaration, 2018).

**Bangladesh examples**

Community clinics; EPI; ORS and zinc; vitamin A; family planning; DOTS for TB. These essential services drove the large fall in child and maternal deaths since 1990.

## DU.46
**Equitable distribution: meaning**

- First principle of PHC: health services must be shared equally by all people irrespective of their ability to pay; all (rich or poor, urban or rural) must have access.
- **Social equity**: equal access for equal need; more resources to those in greater need.

**Why it is essential**

1. Health services were concentrated in cities and hospitals, where a minority lives → **social injustice**.
2. **Inverse care law** (Tudor Hart, 1971): availability of good care varies inversely with need.
3. The poor and rural carry the **highest burden** of disease and death.
4. Health is a **human right**; HFA and UHC are impossible without equity.
5. Inequity wastes resources: costly tertiary care for few, no basic care for many.
6. Equity improves national indicators fastest (IMR, MMR).

**Implementation in Bangladesh**

1. **Community clinics**: one per ~6,000 rural people, within 30 minutes' walk.
2. **Union-level facilities** and **Upazila Health Complexes** in every upazila.
3. **Free or low-cost services**: EPI, essential drugs, FP methods, TB and malaria treatment.
4. **Outreach**: EPI outreach sites, satellite clinics, domiciliary visits by HA and FWA.
5. **Pro-poor schemes**: maternal health voucher scheme; Shasthyo Surokhsha Karmasuchi (health card for the poor).
6. **Urban PHC** for slum dwellers through city corporations and NGOs.
7. **Special areas**: hill tracts, haor, char and coastal areas.
8. **Gender equity**: women-friendly services, female health workers.

**Problems**

Vacant posts in rural facilities, high out-of-pocket spending, urban–rural gap.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Health Care of the Community).

## DU.47
**Equal vs equitable distribution**

| Point | Equal distribution | Equitable distribution |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Same amount of resources to everyone | Resources according to **need** |
| Basis | Sameness | Fairness / justice |
| Assumption | Everyone starts with the same needs | Needs differ between people and areas |
| Effect on gaps | Gaps persist or widen | Gaps narrow |
| Concept | Equality | Equity |
| PHC principle | Not sufficient | **First principle of PHC** |

**Illustrations**

1. **Doctors per upazila**: equal = same number for every upazila; equitable = more staff for remote haor, char and hill upazilas with worse indicators.
2. **Iron-folic acid**: equal = same tablets for all women; equitable = extra supply to pregnant and anaemic women.
3. **Food support**: equal = same ration to every child; equitable = therapeutic food to severely malnourished children.
4. **Health budget**: equal per head ignores need; equitable gives more to districts with high IMR and MMR.
5. **Hospital beds**: equal per city and village is unrealistic; equitable ensures every person can reach basic care (community clinic within 30 minutes' walk).

**Key point**

Equity = equal access for equal need + more for greater need → fair health outcomes.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Health Care of the Community).

## DU.48
**Definition**

**Appropriate technology**: technology that is scientifically sound, adaptable to local needs, and acceptable to those who apply it and to those for whom it is used, and that can be maintained by the people themselves, using resources the community and country can afford (Park).

**Criteria**

1. Scientifically sound and effective
2. Simple; usable by community health workers or families
3. Low cost, affordable
4. Acceptable (social, cultural)
5. Uses local resources; maintainable by people
6. Safe

**Examples**

| Area | Appropriate technology |
|---|---|
| Diarrhoea | ORS, home-made salt–sugar (lobon-gur) solution, zinc |
| Nutrition | Growth chart, MUAC tape, iodised salt |
| Maternal health | Clean delivery kit, misoprostol for PPH, partograph |
| Newborn care | Kangaroo mother care, chlorhexidine for cord care |
| Water and sanitation | Tube well, chlorination, sanitary pit latrine |
| Immunisation | Cold chain (ice-lined refrigerators, vaccine carriers), VVM |

**Home-made ORS: a classic example**

1. **Scientifically sound**: glucose-coupled sodium absorption replaces water and salt lost in diarrhoea.
2. **Simple**: a pinch of salt, a fistful of gur or sugar, in about half a litre of clean water; any mother can make it.
3. **Low cost**: uses salt, gur and water found in every home.
4. **Acceptable**: familiar ingredients; given by the mother at home.
5. **Available immediately**: no need to wait for packets or a health facility.
6. **Effective**: prevents dehydration deaths; Bangladesh scaled it up nationally through mass teaching of mothers.

| PHC element | Role in diarrhoea |

| Health education | Hand-washing with soap, food hygiene, safe disposal of child faeces, home-made ORS, danger signs |
| Food and nutrition | Exclusive breastfeeding to 6 months, complementary feeding, continued feeding during diarrhoea, vitamin A |
| Safe water and sanitation | Tube wells, water treatment, sanitary latrines, ending open defecation |
| MCH | IMCI; care of young children; growth monitoring |
| Immunisation | Measles vaccine (reduces post-measles diarrhoea); oral cholera vaccine in outbreaks and camps |
| Local endemic disease control | Cholera surveillance, outbreak response |
| Treatment of common diseases | **ORS + zinc** + continued feeding; antibiotics only for dysentery and cholera; IV fluids for severe dehydration |
| Essential drugs | ORS packets, zinc tablets at community clinics and UHCs |

**Prevention, control and treatment**

1. **Prevention**: water, sanitation, hygiene (WASH), breastfeeding, nutrition, immunisation.
2. **Control**: surveillance, early detection of outbreaks, chlorination, health education.
3. **Treatment**: case management at home (ORS), community clinics (IMCI), UHC and hospitals (severe cases).

**Principles applied**

Community participation (mothers making ORS); appropriate technology (ORS, zinc); intersectoral coordination (DPHE, local government); equitable distribution (free ORS).

**Bangladesh**

ORS pioneered by icddr,b; by 2014 about 80% of children with diarrhoea received ORS and 34% ORS + zinc.

## DU.61
**What it is**

**Health Survey and Development Committee** (India), 1943–1946, chaired by **Sir Joseph Bhore**; reviewed health conditions of undivided British India (including present Bangladesh) and planned future health services. Report: 1946.

**Concept**

Health services should be **comprehensive**, **integrated** (preventive + curative) and reach the **rural** population through health centres; doctors should be **social physicians**.

**Objectives**

1. Survey the existing health situation and organisation.
2. Recommend future development of health services.

**Recommendations**

1. **Integration of preventive and curative services** at all administrative levels.
2. **Primary health centres** in two stages:
   - **Short-term**: one PHC per 40,000 population, with 2 doctors, 1 nurse, 4 public health nurses, 4 midwives, 4 trained dais, 2 sanitary inspectors, 2 health assistants, 1 pharmacist and 15 class IV staff; secondary health centres to support and supervise PHCs.
   - **Long-term ("3 million plan")**: primary health units with 75-bed hospitals for 10,000–20,000 people; secondary units with 650-bed hospitals; district hospitals with 2,500 beds.
3. **Medical education**: 3 months' training in preventive and social medicine to prepare "**social physicians**".

**Significance**

Basis of rural health centres and PHC in the subcontinent; precursor of thana (upazila) health complexes; introduced the idea of comprehensive health care.

## DU.62

## DU.63
**Meaning**

**Natural history of disease**: the way a disease evolves over time in a person, from the earliest stage of its pre-pathogenesis to its termination as recovery, disability or death, **without any treatment or intervention**.

**Phases**

| Phase | Features |
|---|---|
| **1. Pre-pathogenesis** | Before man is involved; disease agent present in the environment; interaction of **agent, host and environment** creates the stimulus; person is "at risk" |
| **2. Pathogenesis** | Begins with entry of the agent into the host; agent multiplies, tissue changes occur |

**Stages of pathogenesis**

Stimulus → Early pathogenesis (incubation / pre-symptomatic) → Early discernible lesions → Advanced disease → Convalescence → Outcome (recovery, disability, defect, chronic state, death)

- **Clinical horizon**: line separating subclinical from clinical disease.
- Infectious diseases: incubation period; chronic diseases: latent period.

**Levels of prevention along natural history (Leavell and Clark)**

| Stage | Level | Mode of intervention |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-pathogenesis | Primary prevention | Health promotion; specific protection |
| Early pathogenesis | Secondary prevention | Early diagnosis and treatment |
| Late pathogenesis | Tertiary prevention | Disability limitation; rehabilitation |

**Implications / uses**

1. Basis of the **levels of prevention**.
2. Helps plan **screening** (detect early, pre-symptomatic disease).
3. Shows where the **iceberg** (hidden disease) lies.
4. Natural history of chronic diseases is best studied by **cohort studies**.
5. Helps predict prognosis and evaluate interventions.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Concept of Health and Disease).

## DU.64
**Definition**

**Miasmatic theory**: the belief that disease is caused by **"miasma"** (bad air), foul-smelling, poisonous vapours rising from decaying organic matter, filth, sewage, marshes and swamps.

**Features**

1. Prevailed from ancient times to the late 19th century (before the germ theory).
2. Disease attributed to environment and smell, not to specific organisms.
3. Name **malaria** comes from Italian *mal aria*, "bad air".
4. Cholera, plague and typhus were blamed on miasma.

**Contribution**

1. Led to the **sanitary movement**: cleaning streets, drainage, sewage disposal, safe water (Chadwick, Public Health Act 1848; Florence Nightingale).
2. These measures reduced disease even though the theory was wrong.

**Decline**

Disproved by John Snow (cholera via water, 1854) and the **germ theory** (Pasteur, Koch).

**Theories of disease causation (sequence)**

Supernatural → Theory of humours → **Miasmatic** → Germ theory → Epidemiological triad → Multifactorial causation (web of causation)

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Concept of Health and Disease).

## DU.65
**Multifactorial causation**

- Disease results from the **interaction of many factors** (agent, host, environment, lifestyle), not a single cause.
- Early proponent: **Pettenkofer** of Munich (19th century).
- **Web of causation** (MacMahon and Pugh): a chain of interlinked factors; removing some links can prevent disease.
- Especially relevant to **non-communicable diseases**; also explains infections (agent is necessary but not sufficient).

**Examples**

| Disease | Factors |
|---|---|
| Coronary heart disease | Hyperlipidaemia, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, obesity, physical inactivity, stress, family history |
| Tuberculosis | *M. tuberculosis* + poverty, malnutrition, overcrowding, HIV, diabetes, smoking |
| Road traffic accident | Driver (alcohol, fatigue), vehicle, road, weather, law enforcement |
| Lung cancer | Smoking, air pollution, occupational exposures, genetic factors |

**Implications**

Prevention can act on any modifiable factor; multiple interventions together give best results.

**Self-care**

- **Definition (WHO, 2019)**: the ability of individuals, families and communities to promote health, prevent disease, maintain health, and cope with illness and disability with or without the support of a health worker.
- Park: activities people undertake to promote their own health, prevent disease, limit illness and restore health, using their own knowledge and skills.

**Examples**

1. Observing symptoms and signs (fever, lump in breast).
2. Self-medication for minor illness (paracetamol, ORS).
3. Self-monitoring: blood pressure, blood glucose, weight.
4. Self-tests: pregnancy test, HIV self-test.
5. Dressing minor wounds; first aid.
6. Healthy lifestyle: diet, exercise, no tobacco.
7. Adherence to treatment in chronic diseases (insulin self-injection).

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Concept of Health and Disease).

## DU.66
**Epidemiological (ecological) triad**

Disease results from interaction of three components:

Agent ↔ Host ↔ Environment

| Component | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Agent** | Factor whose presence (or absence) is needed for disease | Biological (bacteria, viruses), nutrient, physical, chemical, mechanical, social agents |
| **Host** | Human (or animal) who harbours the disease | Age, sex, genetics, immunity, nutrition, behaviour, occupation |
| **Environment** | All external conditions influencing agent and host | Physical (climate, water), biological (vectors, animals), psychosocial (poverty, customs) |

**Role in disease causation**

1. Health = **equilibrium** among agent, host and environment.
2. Disease = **imbalance**: more virulent agent, more susceptible host, or environment favouring agent.
3. Environment links agent and host (e.g., stagnant water → mosquitoes → malaria).
4. Model suits **communicable diseases**; for NCDs the **web of causation** is preferred.
5. Prevention can act on any arm: kill agent, protect host, modify environment.

**Example: cholera**

Agent: *Vibrio cholerae* | Host: low gastric acidity, malnutrition, blood group O | Environment: contaminated water, poor sanitation, floods.

**Modes of transmission**

| Direct | Indirect |
|---|---|
| Direct contact (STIs, skin infections) | Vehicle-borne (water, food, blood) |
| Droplet infection (measles, influenza) | Vector-borne: mechanical, biological (propagative, cyclo-propagative, cyclo-developmental) |
| Contact with soil (tetanus, hookworm) | Airborne (droplet nuclei, dust) |
| Inoculation into skin or mucosa (rabies) | Fomite-borne (towels, utensils) |
| Transplacental (rubella, HIV, syphilis) | Unclean hands and fingers |

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Concept of Health and Disease; Principles of Epidemiology).

## DU.67
**Definition**

**Disease agent**: a substance, living or non-living, or a force, tangible or intangible, whose excessive presence or relative lack may initiate or perpetuate a disease process.

**Classification of agents (Park)**

| Agent | Examples |
|---|---|
| 1. **Biological** | Viruses (measles), rickettsiae (typhus), bacteria (TB), fungi (candida), protozoa (malaria), metazoa (hookworm) |
| 2. **Nutrient** | Deficiency or excess of protein, fat, carbohydrate, vitamins, minerals, water (kwashiorkor, rickets, obesity) |
| 3. **Physical** | Heat, cold, humidity, pressure, radiation, electricity, sound (heat stroke, deafness) |
| 4. **Chemical: endogenous** | Accumulated urea (uraemia), bilirubin (jaundice), ketones, uric acid (gout), calcium carbonate (stones) |
| 4. **Chemical: exogenous** | Allergens, metals (arsenic, lead), fumes, dust, gases, pesticides |
| 5. **Mechanical** | Chronic friction, injuries, fractures, sprains, accidents |
| 6. **Absence or insufficiency or excess of a factor necessary to health** | Hormones (insulin → diabetes), enzymes, chromosomal factors (Down syndrome), immunological factors (agammaglobulinaemia), structure (congenital heart defect) |
| 7. **Social** | Poverty, smoking, alcohol and drug abuse, unhealthy lifestyle, social isolation, maternal deprivation |

**Responsible agent vs sufficient cause**

An agent is usually **necessary but not sufficient**; host and environment decide whether disease develops.

**Clinical vs community medicine (brief)**

Clinical medicine finds the agent in a patient and treats it; community medicine studies agents, host and environment in populations and controls their spread.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Concept of Health and Disease).

## DU.68
**Definition**

**Risk factor**: an attribute or exposure that is significantly associated with the development of a disease (Park). Its presence increases the probability of disease; its removal reduces it.

**Features**

1. Shows **association**, not always proof of cause.
2. Detected by **epidemiological studies** (case-control, cohort).
3. Usually several risk factors act together (multifactorial causation).
4. Many are **modifiable** → basis of prevention.

**Types**

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Modifiable | Smoking, high BP, high cholesterol, obesity, physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, alcohol |
| Non-modifiable | Age, sex, family history, genetic make-up, ethnicity |

**Related terms**

- **Risk**: probability that an event (disease) will occur.
- **Risk marker / indicator**: attribute linked with increased risk but not causal (e.g., low social class).
- **Risk group**: group of people at higher risk (e.g., pregnant women, under-fives, elderly, health workers for hepatitis B).
- **Risk factor vs cause**: a cause is shown to produce disease; a risk factor may be causal or only associated.

**Uses**

1. Predict who will get disease (**risk prediction**).
2. Target **high-risk** groups for screening and prevention.
3. **Primordial and primary prevention** (tobacco control, salt reduction).
4. Plan health services and education.

**Examples by disease**

CHD: smoking, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia | Cervical cancer: HPV, early marriage, multiparity | TB: HIV, malnutrition, diabetes.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Concept of Health and Disease).

## DU.69
**Concept**

The **disease cycle (chain of infection)**: the sequence of links by which an infectious agent leaves one host and infects another. Infection continues only if every link is present; **breaking any link stops transmission**.

**Six stages (links)**

Infectious agent → Reservoir / source → Portal of exit → Mode of transmission → Portal of entry → Susceptible host → (back to agent/reservoir)

| Stage | Meaning | Example (TB) | Control measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Infectious agent | Organism causing disease | *M. tuberculosis* | Early diagnosis, treatment (DOTS) |
| 2. Reservoir / source | Where the agent lives and multiplies | Sputum-positive patient | Case finding, isolation, treatment |
| 3. Portal of exit | Route by which agent leaves | Respiratory tract (cough) | Cough etiquette, masks |
| 4. Mode of transmission | How agent reaches new host | Droplet nuclei (airborne) | Ventilation, sunlight, UV |
| 5. Portal of entry | Route into new host | Respiratory tract | Masks (N95) for health workers |
| 6. Susceptible host | Person who can be infected | Malnourished, HIV-positive | BCG, nutrition, TB preventive therapy |

**Uses**

1. Explains how infection spreads.
2. Identifies the **weakest link** to target control.
3. Guides **infection prevention** in hospitals (hand hygiene, sterilisation, isolation).
4. Basis of the three control approaches: **control of reservoir, interruption of transmission, protection of susceptible**.
5. Helps outbreak investigation and health education.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Principles of Epidemiology; Concept of Health and Disease).

## DU.70
**Iceberg of disease**

A concept comparing disease in a community to an iceberg:

| Part | Represents |
|---|---|
| **Floating tip** (visible) | Clinical cases seen by doctors |
| **Waterline** | Line between apparent and inapparent disease |
| **Submerged mass** (hidden, larger) | Undiagnosed cases, subclinical (inapparent) infections, carriers, latent and pre-symptomatic disease |

**Examples**

Hypertension, diabetes, anaemia, malnutrition, mental illness, TB, polio, hepatitis B; hidden cases far outnumber diagnosed ones.
Iceberg is absent or small in rabies, measles and tetanus (almost all infections are clinical).

**Epidemiological importance**

1. Clinicians see only the tip; the true **burden** is much larger.
2. The hidden part is a major **source of infection** (carriers, subclinical cases) → spread continues unnoticed.
3. Justifies **screening** and **active case finding** to detect hidden cases early (secondary prevention).
4. Hospital data alone **underestimate** disease; community surveys are needed (**community diagnosis**).
5. Guides **planning and resource allocation**.
6. Explains why disease **control is difficult** for diseases with large submerged parts.
7. Underlines community medicine's focus on the **whole population**, not only patients.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Concept of Health and Disease).

## DU.71
**Definition**

**Opportunistic infection**: infection by an organism that usually does not cause disease (or causes only mild disease) in a healthy person, but causes disease when the host's defences are weakened, i.e., it takes the "opportunity" of lowered immunity.

**Conditions that lower immunity**

HIV/AIDS; cancer chemotherapy; organ transplant (immunosuppressants); long-term steroids; diabetes; severe malnutrition; extremes of age; congenital immunodeficiency.

**Examples**

| Group | Organism / disease |
|---|---|
| Bacterial | Tuberculosis, *Mycobacterium avium* complex, recurrent bacterial pneumonia |
| Fungal | Oral and oesophageal candidiasis, *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia, cryptococcal meningitis, histoplasmosis |
| Viral | CMV retinitis, herpes zoster, HSV ulcers, HHV-8 (Kaposi's sarcoma) |
| Protozoal | Cerebral toxoplasmosis, cryptosporidiosis |

**Importance**

1. Main cause of illness and death in **AIDS**; some are AIDS-defining.
2. **TB** is the commonest opportunistic infection in people with HIV in South Asia.

**Prevention**

Antiretroviral therapy; cotrimoxazole prophylaxis; TB preventive therapy; vaccination; good nutrition; infection control in hospitals.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Epidemiology of Communicable Diseases).

## DU.72
**Host factors (intrinsic factors)**

| Group | Factors | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Demographic** | Age, sex, ethnicity | Measles in children; breast cancer in women; thalassaemia in certain groups |
| 2. **Biological** | Genetic factors; biochemical levels (cholesterol); blood groups; physiological state (pregnancy, puberty, stress, fatigue); immunity and previous disease; nutrition | Sickle-cell trait protects against malaria; blood group O and cholera; pregnancy and anaemia |
| 3. **Social and economic** | Socio-economic status, education, occupation, stress, marital status, housing | Silicosis in stone crushers; TB with poverty and overcrowding |
| 4. **Lifestyle** | Personality traits, living habits, nutrition, physical exercise, use of alcohol, drugs, smoking, behaviour | CHD with smoking and inactivity; HIV with risky sexual behaviour |

**Other host factors**

- **Immune status**: vaccination, natural immunity.
- **Personal hygiene**: scabies, worm infestation.
- **Psychological factors**: personality type A and CHD.

**Importance**

Host factors decide **susceptibility**; they explain why only some exposed persons fall ill and help identify **high-risk groups** for prevention.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Concept of Health and Disease).

## DU.73
**Definitions**

1. **Webster**: a condition in which body health is impaired; a departure from a state of health; an alteration of the body that interrupts performance of vital functions.
2. **Oxford English Dictionary**: a condition of the body, or of some part or organ, in which its functions are disturbed or deranged.
3. **Ecological view**: maladjustment of the human organism to its environment.

**Disease, illness, sickness**

| Term | Meaning | Perspective |
|---|---|---|
| **Disease** | Physiological or psychological dysfunction | Doctor's (objective) |
| **Illness** | Subjective state of feeling unwell | Patient's (subjective) |
| **Sickness** | State of social dysfunction; role the person takes when ill ("sick role") | Society's |

A person may have disease without illness (hypertension) or illness without disease (anxiety).

**Features**

- Has a cause (agent, host, environment interaction).
- Follows a **natural history**.
- Has a spectrum: inapparent → mild → severe → fatal.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Concept of Health and Disease).

## DU.74
**Definition**

**Disease control**: ongoing operations aimed at reducing:
1. the **incidence** of disease,
2. the **duration** of disease and so the risk of transmission,
3. the **effects** of infection, including physical and psychosocial complications,
4. the **financial burden** to the community.

The aim is to reduce the disease to a level where it is **no longer a public health problem**; the agent continues to exist in the community.

**Spectrum**

Control → Elimination → Eradication

**Measures**

| Approach | Examples |
|---|---|
| Control of reservoir | Early diagnosis, treatment, isolation, quarantine, notification |
| Interruption of transmission | Water purification, sanitation, vector control, hand hygiene, safe sex |
| Protection of susceptible | Immunisation, chemoprophylaxis, nutrition, personal protection |
| Surveillance | Notification, case detection, outbreak response, monitoring of trends |

Supported by health education, intersectoral action and community participation.

**Examples (Bangladesh)**

TB (DOTS), malaria (bed nets, rapid tests), dengue (source reduction, case management), diarrhoea (ORS, WASH).

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Concept of Health and Disease).

## DU.75
**Levels of prevention and modes of intervention**

| Level | Phase of natural history | Mode of intervention | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primordial** | Before risk factors appear | Prevent emergence of risk factors (individual and mass education, policy) | Discourage children from smoking; promote healthy diet and play; tobacco laws |
| **Primary** | Pre-pathogenesis | 1. **Health promotion** | Health education, nutrition, sanitation, housing, lifestyle change |
| | | 2. **Specific protection** | Immunisation, chemoprophylaxis, iodised salt, vitamin A, helmets, protective gear, condom use |
| **Secondary** | Early pathogenesis | 3. **Early diagnosis and treatment** | Screening (BP, diabetes, VIA), case finding (TB), treatment of malaria, IMCI |
| **Tertiary** | Late pathogenesis | 4. **Disability limitation** | Good diabetes control to prevent amputation; treatment of stroke; leprosy MDT and care |
| | | 5. **Rehabilitation** | Medical, vocational, social, psychological: physiotherapy, artificial limb, job training |

**Key points**

1. Levels follow the **natural history of disease** (Leavell and Clark).
2. Five modes of intervention: health promotion, specific protection, early diagnosis and treatment, disability limitation, rehabilitation.
3. Earlier prevention is cheaper and more effective.
4. Secondary prevention is often the main option for diseases without primary prevention.

**Example: diabetes at all levels**

Primordial: healthy school food | Primary: weight control, exercise | Secondary: blood glucose screening | Tertiary: foot care, eye laser, rehabilitation after amputation.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Concept of Health and Disease).

## DU.76
**Definition**

**Primordial prevention**: prevention of the emergence or development of **risk factors** in countries or population groups in which they have not yet appeared.

**Features**

1. Newest level; acts **before** risk factors appear.
2. Especially for **chronic NCDs** (CHD, hypertension, diabetes, cancers).
3. Targets **children and young people**, before harmful habits form.
4. Works mainly through **individual and mass education** and healthy public policy.
5. Population-wide; not disease-specific.

**Examples**

| Risk factor prevented | Measure |
|---|---|
| Smoking | Tobacco control law, school education, ban on advertising, taxes |
| Unhealthy diet | School nutrition, limits on trans fat, salt and sugar; healthy food labelling |
| Physical inactivity | Playgrounds, parks, safe walking and cycling paths |
| Obesity | Breastfeeding promotion, healthy lifestyle education |
| Alcohol and drugs | Laws, awareness, family support |
| Air pollution | Clean fuel, emission control |

**Primordial vs primary prevention**

| Primordial | Primary |
|---|---|
| Prevents risk factors | Prevents disease in those with/without risk factors |
| Before risk factors appear | Pre-pathogenesis phase |
| Mainly policy and education | Health promotion + specific protection |

**Bangladesh examples**

Smoking and Tobacco Products Usage (Control) Act 2005 (amended 2013); school health education; anti-tobacco campaigns.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Concept of Health and Disease).

## DU.77
**Definition**

**Primary prevention**: action taken **before the onset of disease** that removes the possibility that the disease will ever occur; intervention in the **pre-pathogenesis** phase.

**Modes of intervention**

**1. Health promotion** (not disease-specific)
- Health education
- Environmental modifications (safe water, sanitation, housing)
- Nutritional interventions (food fortification, supplementation)
- Lifestyle and behavioural changes

**2. Specific protection** (disease-specific)
- Immunisation (EPI: BCG, pentavalent, MR, PCV)
- Use of specific nutrients (iodised salt, vitamin A, iron-folic acid)
- Chemoprophylaxis (antimalarials for travellers, cotrimoxazole in HIV)
- Protection against occupational hazards (masks, earplugs)
- Protection against accidents (helmets, seat belts)
- Protection from carcinogens (HPV vaccine, no tobacco)
- Avoidance of allergens
- Control of specific environmental hazards (air pollution)
- Quality control of food, drugs and cosmetics

**Approaches**

| Approach | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Population (mass) strategy** | Lower the risk factor level in the whole population | Salt reduction for all |
| **High-risk strategy** | Target persons at special risk | Statins and counselling for high-risk adults |

**Importance**

Most effective and cheapest level; "prevention is better than cure"; WHO and Alma-Ata stress it.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Concept of Health and Disease).

## DU.78
**Definitions**

| Term | Meaning | Measures needed after achievement | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Control** | Reduction of incidence, prevalence, morbidity or mortality to an acceptable level | Continued | TB, malaria |
| **Elimination as a public health problem** (WHO) | Achievement of defined measurable targets (e.g., below a threshold rate) in an area | Continued | Kala-azar: <1 case/10,000 at upazila level |
| **Elimination of disease** | Reduction to **zero incidence** of disease in a defined geographical area | Continued | Neonatal tetanus in a country |
| **Elimination of infection** | Reduction to **zero incidence of infection** in a defined area | Continued | Polio in a region |
| **Eradication** | Permanent reduction to **zero worldwide incidence of infection**; termination of all transmission by extermination of the agent | **Not needed** | Smallpox |
| **Extinction** | Agent no longer exists in nature or laboratory | None | None yet |

**Eradication: smallpox**

- Last natural case worldwide: Somalia, 1977; Bangladesh's last case: 1975.
- WHO declared global eradication in **1980**.
- Candidates: poliomyelitis, dracunculiasis (guinea worm), yaws.

**Elimination: Bangladesh**

1. **Lymphatic filariasis**: eliminated as a public health problem (WHO validated, May 2023).
2. **Kala-azar**: eliminated as a public health problem (WHO validated, October 2023); first country in the world.
3. **Polio**: free, with SEAR certified polio-free in 2014.

**Key difference**

Elimination = regional, control measures must continue; eradication = global, measures can stop.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Concept of Health and Disease); WHO, 2023, https://www.who.int/news/item/31-10-2023-bangladesh-achieves-historic-milestone-by-eliminating-kala-azar-as-a-public-health-problem

## DU.81
**Definition**

**Community diagnosis**: identification and quantification of the health problems of a community in terms of mortality and morbidity rates and ratios, and identification of their correlates (determinants), in order to define those at risk or in need of health care.

It is to the community what clinical diagnosis is to the patient.

**Steps**

Collect data (census, records, surveys) → Calculate rates and ratios → Identify main problems and at-risk groups → Find causes / correlates → Set priorities → Plan action (community treatment)

**Data used**

Demographic (age-sex structure), vital statistics (births, deaths), morbidity, nutritional status, environment (water, sanitation), socio-economic status, health services and their use.

**Example**

A village survey shows high diarrhoea and stunting among under-fives, linked with unsafe water, open defecation and poor feeding → diagnosis: "diarrhoea and undernutrition due to poor WASH and feeding practices".

**Importance in prevention of disease**

1. **Identifies priority problems and their causes** → targets prevention where it is needed most.
2. **Defines high-risk groups** (mothers, under-fives, elderly, workers) → selective protection and screening.
3. **Basis for planning and resource allocation** of preventive programmes (immunisation, WASH, nutrition).
4. **Baseline for evaluation**: later surveys show whether prevention worked.
5. Detects the **hidden iceberg** of disease missed by hospital data.
6. Promotes **community participation** in solving local problems.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Man and Medicine; Principles of Epidemiology).

## DU.82
**Definition**

**Community treatment**: organised, collective health action taken by the health system and the community to deal with the health problems identified by **community diagnosis**; it includes promotive, preventive, curative and rehabilitative measures applied to the whole population or groups.

Community diagnosis → Community treatment → Evaluation

**Methods**

1. Health education and behaviour change.
2. Environmental measures: safe water, sanitation, vector control.
3. Mass immunisation campaigns.
4. **Mass drug administration** and chemoprophylaxis.
5. Nutrition programmes: supplementation, fortification.
6. Screening and case finding.
7. Legislation: food safety, tobacco control.
8. Organisation of health services and referral.

**Role in controlling disease**

| Disease | Community treatment |
|---|---|
| Lymphatic filariasis | Annual MDA with DEC + albendazole → eliminated in Bangladesh (2023) |
| Polio | National Immunisation Days |
| Cholera | Chlorination, ORS, oral cholera vaccine campaigns |
| Worm infestation | Mass deworming of school children |
| Vitamin A deficiency | Biannual vitamin A campaigns |
| Dengue | Community source reduction |
| Iodine deficiency | Universal salt iodisation |

**Clinical vs community treatment**

| Clinical | Community |
|---|---|
| Individual patient | Whole population |
| Drugs, surgery | Programmes, policy, environment |
| Cure | Prevention and control |
| Evaluated by recovery | Evaluated by fall in incidence |

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Man and Medicine).

## DU.83
**Definition**

**Screening**: the search for unrecognised disease or defect by means of rapidly applied tests, examinations or other procedures in apparently healthy individuals. It sorts out those who **probably have** the disease from those who probably do not; it is **not diagnostic** (positives need diagnostic confirmation) (US Commission on Chronic Illness).

**Classification (types of screening)**

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Mass screening** | Whole population or large group, regardless of risk | Hypertension screening of all adults in a community |
| **High-risk / selective screening** | Only groups at higher risk | Diabetes in obese adults; cervical cancer screening in women 30–60 years |
| **Multiphasic screening** | Several tests at one time | Health check-up: BP, glucose, lipids, Hb, urine |
| **Opportunistic (case finding)** | Testing patients who come for other reasons | Checking BP of every OPD patient |

**By purpose (uses)**

1. **Case detection** (prescriptive screening): for the person's benefit (neonatal hypothyroidism, breast cancer).
2. **Control of disease** (prospective screening): to protect others (TB, STIs in sex workers, HBsAg in blood donors).
3. **Research**: natural history of chronic disease.
4. **Educational opportunity**: raising awareness.

**Criteria of a good screening test**

Acceptability; repeatability (reliability); validity (sensitivity, specificity); yield; simplicity; safety; rapidity; ease of administration; low cost.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Screening for Disease).

## DU.84
**2 × 2 table**

| Screening test | Disease present | Disease absent | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Positive | a (true positive) | b (false positive) | a + b |
| Negative | c (false negative) | d (true negative) | c + d |
| Total | a + c | b + d | N |

**Sensitivity vs specificity**

| Point | Sensitivity | Specificity |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Ability of a test to identify correctly those **who have** the disease | Ability of a test to identify correctly those **who do not have** the disease |
| Formula | a / (a + c) × 100 | d / (b + d) × 100 |
| Also called | True positive rate | True negative rate |
| Linked error | Low sensitivity → more **false negatives** | Low specificity → more **false positives** |
| Effect of error | Cases missed; false reassurance; disease spreads | Healthy people investigated, anxious, extra cost |
| Preferred when | Disease serious, treatable, missing it is costly (e.g., blood donor HIV, PKU) | False positives are harmful or costly (e.g., before surgery or toxic treatment); disease rare |
| Effect of moving cut-off (e.g., lowering glucose cut-off) | Increases | Decreases |

**Relationship**

- Sensitivity and specificity are **inversely related** when the cut-off changes; both are inherent properties of the test, not affected by prevalence.
- Two tests in **series** raise specificity; in **parallel** raise sensitivity.

**Related measures**

Positive predictive value = a / (a + b); negative predictive value = d / (c + d); these depend on prevalence.

**Example**

100 diseased, 900 healthy; test positive in 90 diseased and 45 healthy → sensitivity = 90/100 = 90%; specificity = 855/900 = 95%.

**Ref:** Park K. Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine, 28th ed. (Screening for Disease).
